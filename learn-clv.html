CLV

Closing line value is the only honest scoreboard in sports betting — it tells you whether your bet beat the final price, and over a few hundred bets, it's the single best predictor of long-run profit. Most bettors track P&L. The ones who track CLV know whether their P&L will last.

What is it?

You can place a hundred +EV bets and still be down 8 units after a month. Variance is real. So how do you know — actually know — that you're a winning bettor and not just running good or running bad?

CLV. Closing Line Value.

The closing line is the price the market lands on right before tip-off. It represents the sharpest, most-informed estimate the entire market could come up with — every sharp bettor, every model, every piece of late-breaking information baked in. By the time the line closes, almost all the noise has been squeezed out.

If you bet a line and it moves toward you by the time it closes — i.e. you got a price the closing market thinks was too good for you — you got positive CLV. You beat the close. Beating the close, over and over, is the only reliable proof you're +EV.

The math behind it

You bet Mavs +6.5 at −110. By tip-off, the line has moved to Mavs +5.5 −110. The closing market is paying you on a number worse than what you got. Devig the close to get the fair price the market thinks +6.5 is worth — say, −125. Now compare:

Your CLV on this bet Price you got : −110 (decimal 1.91)
Fair price at close: −125 (decimal 1.80)
────────────────────────────────────
CLV = (1.91 / 1.80) − 1 = +6.4%

The bet itself might lose. The Mavs might cover by 1, or not at all. CLV doesn't care. It's measuring the quality of the price you got, not the outcome.

Why it matters more than you think

A bettor who's running +5% CLV over 200 bets is almost certainly a winning bettor — even if they're sitting at −3 units on the year. Variance just hasn't caught up yet.

A bettor who's running −2% CLV over 200 bets is almost certainly a losing bettor — even if they're sitting at +12 units on the year. They got lucky. Variance always catches up.

CLV is the only short-term metric that doesn't lie. Profit is too noisy. Win rate is too noisy. CLV is signal — at the cost of being slightly less satisfying than counting your winnings on a hot week.

What sharps do about it

They review every bet at close. They track CLV obsessively, not unit profit. If their CLV is positive, they keep doing what they're doing — even on losing weeks. If their CLV turns negative, they investigate before blaming bad luck.

You can't always win. You can always check whether you're picking sharper than the market — and that's the only thing that tells you if winning is on the way.
