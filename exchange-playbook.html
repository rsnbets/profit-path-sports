01

What a betting exchange is — and why it matters

An exchange is the only place a winning bettor can actually keep winning. Here's how it works.

A betting exchange — like ProphetX or Novig — is a peer-to-peer market. Instead of betting against a sportsbook, you're betting against another user. The platform just matches the two sides and takes a small commission. Nobody is the "house."

Every bet you see listed on an exchange has been created by one or more other users. It doesn't exist until somebody writes it.

When you click "bet the Lakers at +130," you're not buying a bet from the platform — you're saying yes to a wager somebody (or a stack of somebodies) already posted at that price. The bigger the dollar amount sitting there, the more people stacked up making that same offer. That's what makes exchanges fundamentally different from a sportsbook — and it's what makes the offers worth reading.

That single difference makes exchanges valuable for two very different kinds of bettor.

For regular bettors: cheaper bets

Exchanges almost always have lower vig than traditional sportsbooks. There's no house position to protect and no marketing budget baked into the price — the market just has to find a clearing price between two users. So the exchange line is usually a few percent closer to the true odds than what FanDuel or DraftKings is offering. Even if you never learn to read the offers, just placing your everyday bets at an exchange tends to save you money per ticket — same wagers, smaller cut.

For sharp bettors: no limits, no flagging

For winning bettors, the bigger draw is what exchanges don't do. They don't cut your limits when you win, and they don't flag your account for being profitable. At a traditional sportsbook, the better you do, the smaller they let you bet — until eventually you're capped at $5 a game or closed entirely. On an exchange, the platform doesn't care whether you win or lose; they make money on commission either way. So sharp bettors can size up properly without their accounts dying.

For anyone: the book is the signal

That second part — sharps can actually bet at full size — is what makes the offers valuable to everyone, including bettors who never trade on the exchange themselves. The sharpest money in the US market gravitates to exchanges precisely because nobody can stop it from getting down. So the offers sitting on the book aren't anonymous noise — they're the closest thing to seeing the sharp market in real time, denominated in dollars. The rest of this playbook teaches you how to read it.

A traditional sportsbook line is a guess about what the market thinks. The offers on an exchange are what the market actually thinks, denominated in dollars.

Quick jargon note before we go further. You'll see two terms a lot: a maker is someone who posts a price (offers a bet to other users), and a taker is someone who accepts a posted price. We'll lean on these only when the signal logic needs them. If you remember nothing else: the depth on your screen was put there by makers — mostly sharps. That's the signal.

  • Lower vig means even casual bettors save money per ticket vs. FanDuel or DraftKings.
  • No flagging, no limit cuts — winning bettors can actually scale up instead of getting capped out.
  • The offers on the screen are the sharp market in real time. Learning to read them is the rest of this playbook.
02

Reading the ProphetX screen

Two things you might see — only one matters here

One quick distinction before the signals. Now that you know every bet on an exchange has to be created by someone before anyone can take it, two different things can show up on the screen.

  • Bets still waiting to be taken. Someone posted "I'll lay the Jays at −115 for $5,000" and nobody has taken them up on it yet, so the offer is sitting there in line. This is the main exchange screen — the price ladder you see on ProphetX, Novig, or in Pick the Odds. (Traders sometimes call this the order book — same thing.)
  • Bets that already happened. Two users met at a price and the bet went through. This is just history — usually shown on a separate "trade history" or "volume" tab. (You'll hear this called matched volume.)

The rest of this playbook is about the first one — bets still waiting. Those tell you what sharps are willing to do right now, with their money posted and waiting for someone to take it. Completed bets are old news; sharp money may have already moved on. When you're deciding whether to make a bet, you want live opinion — not last hour's history.

The mental model: the offers stack up at each price — a wall of bets waiting for somebody to take them. Each offer is somebody waiting for you to hit it. The shape of that wall — where it's thick, where it's thin, where it suddenly collapses — is the signal.

03

The translation rule — flip the side, flip the sign

Most people see a price on an exchange and read it the way they'd read a sportsbook: "the line is X, here's what I'd pay." On an exchange, that reading is backwards. Flip it, and every other signal in this playbook starts making sense.

The rule, in one sentence: every offered price on an exchange is a bet the sharps are trying to trick you into taking. To post it, they had to lock themselves into the opposite side, at the opposite sign of the same magnitude.

Every displayed price gets two operations, run together:

  • Flip the side — if the display says back Eagles, the sharp is on the 49ers.
  • Flip the sign of the price — if the display says −115, the sharp got +115. Same magnitude, opposite sign. Not +100. Not +110. Exactly +115.
Flip the side, flip the sign — worked
DisplayJays −115 · $5,000 available
Sharp's positionRays +115 · $5,000 of conviction
DisplayPistons −110 · $5,800 available
Sharp's positionCavs +110 · $5,800 of conviction
DisplayEagles +125 · $1,200 available
Sharp's position49ers −125 · $1,200 of conviction

Why the rule works

When a sharp posts $5,800 of depth offering Pistons at −110, they didn't post it because they like the Pistons. They posted it because they want it filled — they think Cavs are the better side, and they need a bettor to take the Pistons bait so they can collect on Cavs. The deeper the offer, the harder the sharps are trying to get someone to take that side.

So when you see "Pistons −110 · $5,800 available," translate it instantly: "a sharp is sitting on Cavs +110 with $5,800 of conviction, hoping I take the Pistons bait." Now compare to what FanDuel or BetMGM is offering you for Cavs. That gap is where the cross-market edge lives.

The displayed price is what the sharps are daring you to take. Their real bet is the mirror image — and that's the number that matters.

Now go back to the ProphetX screenshot above. The $2,260 of depth offered to bet LAD at −360 means a sharp is sitting on COL at +360 with $2,260 of conviction. The $118 of depth offered to bet COL at +345 means a sharp is sitting on LAD at −345 with only $118 of conviction. Same screen you couldn't read five minutes ago — now you can.
04

What the book is telling you — the core signals

Once the translation rule clicks, the offers stop being a wall of numbers and starts being a sentence. Six readings carry almost all the signal — listed roughly in order of how often you'll use them. Each one is just the translation rule applied with a different shape of evidence.

Signal 1 — Heavy depth on side A means sharps want side B

The single most useful read on an exchange, and the one most newcomers get backwards. The bigger the block of money offered on a side, the more makers (sharps) are willing to take the opposite side at that price. The bet to make is the side without the heavy maker depth — find it priced softly at a traditional sportsbook and you're alongside the smart money.

Pitcher Ks · Strikeouts Over 6.5
Bet Over −110$5,800 available — a big maker stack
Bet Over −108$1,400 available
Bet Over −105$380 available (thin)
ReadSharps stacked $5,800 of capacity laying Over — they think Under is more likely than 52.4% (the implied prob of −110). If a soft book has Under 6.5 at +100 or +105, that's the +EV bet.

Caveat: very thin books distort this. Side A $80 vs. side B $40 isn't a signal — it's two recreational bettors. You need real depth on at least one side for the read to mean anything.

Signal 2 — Outsized depth on a thin market

The same $4,000 of available liquidity means very different things on different markets. On a Sunday-night NFL moneyline it's noise — that market trades millions, and $4k is small relative to the players involved. On a Tuesday-night NHL backup-goalie save prop, it's loud. Sharp books cap niche markets at $500–$1,000 even for proven sharps; when an exchange shows multiple thousands of maker depth on a market like that, you're looking at sharp money that hit its limit at the sharp books and came to the exchange to deploy more size. Conviction behind that depth is much higher than the same dollars on a mainstream game.

Two ways to read it — same signal:

  • The Pinnacle-limit ratio. Compare exchange depth to the typical sharp-book cap on the market type. 3× = meaningful. 5×+ = rare and worth paying close attention to.
  • The peer-set scan. Faster, no lookup required. Pull up the full board for a category (assist props, pitcher Ks, alt-spread ladders). Note the baseline depth most lines share. Any line where one side runs 3–5×+ that baseline is an asymmetric outlier — concentrated sharp conviction that doesn't apply to the rest of the category.
Peer-set scan — tonight's NBA assist props

Player B · O 6.5$180 / $200 depth
Player C · O 7.5$1,400 / $220 depth — outlier

ReadPeer baseline: ~$200–$300 per side. Player C breaks the pattern by ~5×. The $1,400 is offered to bet the Over — so the sharp is on Under with $1,400 of conviction. Look for Under 7.5 at a soft book.

When both reads are available — outsized vs. Pinnacle and outsized vs. peers — you can size up confidently. Either one alone is meaningfully strong.

Signal 3 — Imbalance: which side has more dollars available

Sum the total available liquidity on each side of the market, across all visible price levels. The side with substantially more money available to back is the side sharps are laying — meaning sharps want the other side. Imbalance is direction, but inverted.

Lakers vs. Warriors moneyline
Available to bet Lakers$11,400 stacked across all visible prices
Available to bet Warriors$2,800 stacked across all visible prices
Read4-to-1 imbalance on the Lakers side. Makers are heavily laying Lakers — they want bettors to take Lakers because they're confident Warriors is the better side. If the traditional-book consensus has Lakers as a slight favorite, the exchange is whispering Warriors are sharper than the consensus implies. The bet to look for is Warriors at a soft book.

Why it works: exchanges have no incentive to balance both sides — there's no house position to protect. A book is imbalanced only when one side has many makers willing to lay it. Read it as a directional clue, not a guarantee — a snapshot can fill in over the next hour as more participants arrive.

Signal 4 — Spread tightness: how settled the market is

The gap between the best offer on side A and the best offer on side B tells you how confident the market is in fair price.

Spread → market confidence
2–5 centsMature market, lots of participants, agreed-upon fair. Treat the midpoint as fair and shop traditional books against it.
5–10 centsNormal exchange spread. Midpoint is approximately fair — go gently.
10–20 centsThin market or recent news; participants disagree. Don't trust either side as fair — wait, or shop traditional books for the soft side.
20¢+ or "no bid"Brand new market, suspended, or nobody cares about it. Avoid. The price could be anywhere from fair to garbage.

The gold standard is a tight spread with deep books — 3-cent spreads with $5,000+ on each side means the sharps have fully priced the market. Take the midpoint as fair and shop traditional books against it. That's when the exchange is screaming the answer loudest.

Signal 5 — Walls and what they sit on

Sometimes one specific price level holds a disproportionately large offer — much bigger than anything else in the book. Traders call these walls. A wall is the translation rule writ large: it's sharps stacking conviction on the opposite side, and they're confident enough in the price level that they're willing to do it deep.

Look back at the ProphetX COL/LAD screenshot above. The biggest single block on the whole page is $2,584 offered to bet LAD at −370. That's the wall. Translate it: a sharp is sitting on COL at +370 with $2,584 of conviction at that level. They're not only confident COL is the sharper side — they're confident enough to put a wall of capacity behind it at one specific deep-stack price. That price is their pivot.

Walls create three tactical options:

  • Bet the opposite side at a soft book. Whatever side the wall is laying, find it at a traditional book before they catch up.
  • Post your own offer in front of the wall. Want to be on the same side as the wall? Post a slightly better price than the wall. If a recreational bettor comes through, you get matched on the sharp side.
  • Don't chase the wall side. Taking the side the wall is offering means you're betting against thousands of dollars of sharp conviction. Possible to be right, but you're on the recreational side by definition.

Signal 6 — Disappearing liquidity = something just happened

One of the most actionable patterns: a book that was deep five minutes ago is suddenly thin or empty on one side. Makers who were willing to lay that side don't want to anymore. Something shifted their model.

A book pulled
10:42 AMPitcher Ks O/U 6.5 — $4,200 available to bet Over at −110 (makers laying Over)
10:43 AMSame market — $0 available to bet Over. Makers pulled their lay offers.
ReadSharps who were happy to lay Over at −110 a minute ago suddenly aren't. They now think Over is more likely than the price implies — they don't want to be short Over anymore. If you can find Over at the old generous price (or close to it) at a soft book, that's the bet.

Common causes for sudden liquidity flight:

  • Lineup change posted — the pitcher got scratched; a star is out; the offense looks more strikeout-friendly
  • Weather report — wind, rain, or snow update changes the model
  • Late-breaking injury news — official report drops; the market hasn't caught up yet
  • Steam from a syndicate — a group of sharps just hit the market hard on one side, scaring off the makers offering it

When liquidity disappears on a side, the side that was being offered is the one that just became sharper.

The signals don't have to agree on their own — they have to agree as a stack. When two or three of the six point the same direction (heavy depth + imbalance + outsized depth, all on the same side), conviction multiplies. When they conflict (imbalance points one way but liquidity just disappeared on the other side), wait — the market is mid-revision and your edge is unstable. Real plays come from stacked signals, not single reads.
