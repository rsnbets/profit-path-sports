The Sharp Exchange Playbook

What the money on the screen can tell you, and what it can't. Reading the book, crossing soft prices, and posting your own.

What you'll have when you're done

  • Six reads on an exchange book, and the traps in each one.
  • How to stack ProphetX, Novig, Kalshi and Polymarket into one picture of the market.
  • The cross-market check: when a sportsbook price beats what exchange makers will accept.
  • The maker play: posting your own price in wide markets, and when not to.
  • A routine you can run in a few minutes per game.

This assumes you have read Exchanges 101, or already read percent odds and know the flip rule: money available on one side means makers are on the other side.

01

What the book can and can't tell you

Exchanges do not limit winning bettors. They earn fees either way. So informed money can bet there at size, and some of it shows up as posted orders. That is why the book is worth reading.

But not every dollar on the screen is a sharp opinion. Three kinds of money sit there:

  • Informed bettors with a view on one side. This is the money you want to read.
  • Market-making firms quoting both sides to earn the spread. Their size says little about direction.
  • The venue itself. Novig adds its own liquidity when a market is thin.

Treat the book as evidence, not proof. One read is a clue. Several reads pointing the same way is a lean. A lean you track against the closing line is how you find out if the clue was worth anything.

Every example below uses percent prices and the flip rule. "$X available on the Over" means makers are on the Under for $X.

02

Six reads on the book

Read 1: one side has far more money available

Add up the money available on each side. When one side is much heavier, makers are leaning the other way.

Lakers vs Warriors moneyline
Available on Lakers$11,400 within 3 points of the best price (56%)
Available on Warriors$2,800 within 3 points of the best price (46%)
ReadMakers are about 4 to 1 on the Warriors. Their price on the Warriors is 44% (100 − 56). A sportsbook offering the Warriors below 44%, about +127 or better, is paying more than those makers asked for.

Count money near the top, not the whole book. Orders posted far from the market are often fishing. They sit there hoping for a careless taker and rarely fill. A useful count is the money within 2 or 3 points of each side's best price. The full-book total is worth a glance, but it is easy to inflate.

Set a floor. $80 against $40 is two people, not a signal. Ignore imbalances where the heavy side is under a few hundred dollars.

Read 2: one line is far deeper than its peers

Pull up every line in one category, such as tonight's assist props. Most will share a normal depth. A line running 3 to 5 times that baseline on one side stands out.

Tonight's assist props · available Over / Under
Player A · 5.5$240 / $310
Player B · 6.5$180 / $200
Player C · 7.5$1,400 / $220
Player D · 4.5$210 / $260
ReadBaseline is $200 to $300 a side. Player C's Over has about 5 times that. Makers are on the Under. Look for Under 7.5 at a soft book.

The same dollars mean more on a small market. $4,000 on a Sunday night NFL moneyline is routine. $4,000 on a midweek hockey saves prop is unusual, because sharp sportsbooks cap those markets at a few hundred dollars.

Read 3: how wide the market is

Add the two best prices. The amount over 100 is the width.

Sum of best pricesWhat it usually meansHow to use it
101% to 102%Busy, agreed marketTreat the exchange fair as a strong reference
103% to 105%Normal for propsFair is a rough guide
106% to 110%Thin, or news is moving itDon't trust either side as fair. Maker opportunities live here
Over 110%, or one side emptyNew, paused, or ignoredSkip it

To get the exchange's fair price, divide each best price by the sum. Over 48% and Under 53% sum to 101%. Fair Over is 48 ÷ 101 = 47.5%. Our boards use the power method instead, which handles longshots better, but on a tight market the two land within a few tenths.

Read 4: money that moves

Money pulled. $4,200 was available on the Over at 52%. A minute later it is gone. The makers who were on the Under at 48% stopped wanting it. Something changed their view: a lineup, an injury report, weather. The Over just got stronger. Check the news, then check whether a soft book still has the old Over price.

Money flooding in. A thin market suddenly gets thousands on both sides and the width drops from 8 points to 2. The market has settled. Sportsbooks often lag that move by minutes.

The catch: a maker can pull an order for reasons that have nothing to do with the game. They hit a risk limit, or their quoting software restarted. One pull is a reason to look, not to bet.

Timing matters. Books are thin and wide when lines first open. The most readable window is a few hours before the game, when real money arrives but the market is not finished. In the last minutes, late news can empty a book fast. Live markets are a different game.

Read 5: walls

A wall is one price level far bigger than the levels around it.

Favorite −2.5 · available to bet the favorite
51%$1,100 (best price)
52%$8,400
53%$1,250
ReadMakers are on the underdog +2.5 at 48% for $8,400. That price is where they are confident. Look for the dog +2.5 at a soft book below 48%.

Walls give you three choices. Bet the dog at a soft book. Post your own order on the dog just ahead of the wall and wait for a taker. Or leave it. Taking the favorite into that wall means betting against the biggest order on the screen.

Read 6: stack every exchange

One exchange can be one trader. The same shape across ProphetX, Novig, Kalshi and Polymarket US is a market. Line up the same market on every venue, at the same line, and add the money at each price.

Sabres at Canadiens · money across exchanges
SabresPlenty available from 44.4% (+125) and up. Almost nothing cheaper.
CanadiensAlmost nothing at 56.5% (−130). Money builds from 57.4% (−135) and up.
ReadThe exchanges won't sell the Sabres below about 44.4% or the Canadiens below about 57.4%. Those add to 101.8%, a tight market. A soft book with the Sabres at +130 or better (43.5%) or the Canadiens at −130 or better (56.5%) is paying more than any exchange will.

Check that every venue is quoting the same line before you add them up. A −2.5 on one app and a −3 on another are different bets.

Stack the reads, not just the dollars. When imbalance, peer depth and a wall all point the same way, you have a lean. When they disagree, the market is still sorting itself out. Wait.
03

The cross-market check

This is where the reads pay off. You are not trying to beat the exchange. You are using it to find a sportsbook price that is out of line.

Dolphins at Panthers · total 44.5
Exchange$16,000 available on the Over at 48.3% (+107). Makers are on the Under at 51.7%.
Hard RockUnder 44.5 at −105, which is 51.2%.
Add them48.3% + 51.2% = 99.5%. Under 100. The two prices are crossed.
ReadHard Rock is selling the Under for less than the makers who posted $16,000 were willing to pay for it. The value is on the Hard Rock Under.

When the sum is under 100%, you could bet both sides and keep a small profit whatever happens, before fees. That is an arbitrage. Most cross-market bettors bet only the sportsbook side, because that is the side priced worse than the exchange.

Near-crosses count too

Prices don't have to cross to be useful. If the soft book is within a point or two of the exchange makers' price, and the exchange market is deep and tight, the soft price is close to fair or better. Decide in advance how close you want. One point is strict. Two points finds more bets with a thinner edge.

What weakens a cross

  • Little money behind it. A cross against a $40 order is probably a stale order, not a real price.
  • A wide exchange market. If the exchange is 108% wide, its price is not a good reference.
  • The sportsbook is moving. Sometimes the book is right and the exchange is slow. If the soft price is changing as you look, it may be the book reacting to news first.
  • Old data. Both prices have to be current. Crosses often close within minutes.
  • Fees. If you hedge on the exchange, use the all-in price for that leg.

A cross means one of two prices is wrong. Usually it is the soft book. Not always. Track every one against the closing line and let the record tell you.

Pick'em apps

Fixed-payout apps like Underdog and PrizePicks pay the same on either side of a prop. So compare the exchange fair price on a side with the app's break-even per pick. If the exchange makes a side clearly more likely than that break-even, it is a candidate leg. The DFS Slip Checker does the break-even math.

04

The maker play: posting your own price

In a wide market, the gap between the two best prices is money left on the table. A taker pays it. A maker can collect it.

Player hits Over 0.5
ExchangeOver available at 60%. Under available at 48%. Sum 108%, so the market is wide.
SportsbooksDe-vigged across books, fair on the Over is about 57%.
PostPost an order to bet the Over at 55%. To a taker it shows as the Under available at 45%.
If it fillsYou hold the Over at 55% against a 57% fair. On Novig or Polymarket US, no fee.

Know the catch

  • It may never fill. That costs you nothing but the bet you didn't make.
  • Fills come at bad times. Your order is most likely to get taken right when news turns against you. A lineup change can make your 55% a gift to the taker.
  • So manage the order. Post closer to game time, watch the news, and cancel when anything changes. Don't leave orders sitting overnight.
  • Your fair price has to come from somewhere else. Use the sportsbook consensus, not the wide exchange midpoint. Posting at the exchange's own midpoint just gets you a coin flip.
  • Fees differ for makers. Free on Novig and Polymarket US. Small on Kalshi sports games. ProphetX takes 2% of winnings either way.
05

Common mistakes, and the routine

Mistakes

  • Reading available money as support for that side. $11,400 available on the Lakers means makers are on the Warriors.
  • Mixing up which price is better. For you, a lower percent is always the better price.
  • Leaving fees out. On a near 50/50 take, a fee-on-price venue adds 1.5 points or more.
  • Trusting thin books. A few hundred dollars on a niche market is one person.
  • Betting more than the top level. Your average price gets worse as you walk the queue.
  • Treating one snapshot as the truth. Books change by the minute. Look twice.
  • Grading on wins. A good bet loses often. Grade on the price you got versus the close.

The routine

  • Pick the market and pull it up on every exchange you can see, at the same line.
  • Check width. Wider than about 106%? Skip it as a reference, or consider posting.
  • Run the reads. Imbalance near the top, peer depth, walls, recent moves.
  • Find the makers' price on the side you lean. Flip the side, subtract from 100.
  • Shop your sportsbooks for a price below it. Crossed or near-crossed?
  • Check what weakens it: thin money, stale data, a moving book.
  • Bet, post, or pass. Then log the price and the close.
