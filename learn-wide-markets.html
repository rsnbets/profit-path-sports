Concept · Maker Strategy

Beating Sharps in Wide Markets

On a wide exchange market, you don't have to take whatever price is showing. You can post your own offer just better than the current best — and get matched first. The maker move for niche props, long-shot markets, and anywhere the spread is loose enough to slide into.

The setup: what "wide" means

Every two-way market has an implied probability sum across its best two prices. On a tight, fully-priced market, that sum is close to 100% — there's no room to slide in between the offers. On a wide market, the sum is meaningfully above 100% (say 102-110%), meaning the gap between the best-back and best-lay prices is large.

Wide markets exist for a reason: they're thinner. Fewer participants. Slower repricing. Less competition between makers. That looseness is the opportunity.

In a tight market, you take whatever's available. In a wide market, you don't take — you post. Squeezing in between the existing offers is where the edge lives.

Read the room

The deep offer is at +500 with $850 on the Over side. That's where some maker sat on a big position. But there's a $32 offer at +510 sitting in front of it — someone slid in at a better price (1.96% better) to be first in line. Same on the Under: the $177 sits at −780, but a $49 offer at −720 is in front of it — also better. Someone is making this play already. They watched a wide market and posted their own offer just under the existing best to capture the next taker.

That's the maker move. Two things made it possible:

  1. The market is wide enough (104.20%) that there's room for a new offer to be inside the existing best without crossing
  2. The existing offers are thin enough ($45-$850) that competition for marginal improvements is realistic
The takeaway: wide markets on niche props are posting opportunities, not taking opportunities. The current best price isn't your ceiling — you can post a slightly better one and let the next backer find you.

What's on the row

  • ProphetX (the sharp/exchange source): Over −219 ($136 depth) / Under +139 ($104 depth)

Compute the ProphetX spread: −219 implied 68.65% + +139 implied 41.84% = 110.49%. That's a very wide market — over 10 cents of vig between the two sides. Per our width table, this is in the "niche/illiquid, post if you have an opinion" tier.

The maker play — swoop in and beat the current best

The current best on ProphetX is +139 to back Under (and −219 to back Over). The spread is 110%, so there's massive room to post inside the existing offers.

Posting the Under at a better price
Current bestUnder +139 with $104 available
Conservative postUnder +150 — slightly better, high fill probability
Moderate postUnder +170 — significantly better, decent fill probability in a thin market
Aggressive postUnder +185 to +195 — close to the bet365 outlier price; needs a backer with a strong Over view
A +170 or +180 fill on Under is way better than the +139 currently sitting at the top of the book. With 110% spread, there's room to move the offered price up by 30-50 cents and still be a competitive offer to backers.

The dual play — bet365 is also a soft outlier

Notice bet365 is way off the consensus on both sides — Over at −280 (much sharper than −220 average) and Under at +202 (much more generous than +139 on PX or +145 on Fanatics). That's a textbook cross-market EV setup separately from the posting tactic:

  • Cross-market EV play: take Under at +202 on bet365. Devig PX's +139/−219 → no-vig fair Under ≈ 37.9% (~+165 American). bet365's +202 implies 33.1% — roughly a 4.8-point probability gap in your favor on a single line.
  • Maker play: post Under on PX at +170-+180 and let a backer find you, capturing the spread without taking the soft-book line.

The two plays aren't mutually exclusive — you could do both if your bankroll splits across the books. Or pick the higher-EV one (bet365's +202 is hard to beat unless you're confident you'll get filled at +180+ on PX).

The lesson: wide markets don't just enable posting — they coexist with cross-market opportunities at soft books. Always scan the full row before deciding whether to take, post, or both.

How to identify a wide market

Look at the implied probability sum across the two best prices. Translate American to implied %, add them up. The sum is your "vig" or "market width":

Market width → tactical implication
Sum of implied probabilitiesWidthTactic
100.0% - 100.5%Razor-tightTake, don't post. No room to improve.
100.5% - 102.0%StandardTake. Minimal posting opportunity.
102.0% - 104.0%WidePost. 1-3 cents better is possible.
104.0% - 107.0%Very widePost aggressively. Big improvement headroom.
107.0%+Niche / illiquidPost if you have an opinion. Otherwise pass.

Where wide markets live, typically:

  • Player props on niche markets — HR props, stolen bases, role-player point totals, anything where the volume is low
  • Long-shot props — first goal scorer, anytime touchdown for backup RB, anything with implied probability below 30%
  • Lower-tier games or leagues — Tuesday-night NHL, midweek WNBA, college props that aren't in OddsJam's main feed
  • Alternate lines — Over 2.5 hits, Over 3.5 strikeouts, anywhere off the mainline
  • Late nights or off hours — markets that don't get the attention of peak-volume windows

How aggressive to undercut

The fundamental trade-off when posting in a wide market:

  • Bigger undercut (e.g., post at +540 when best is +510) → much better fill price if you get matched, BUT lower chance of being matched and you may have to wait longer or watch the market move
  • Smaller undercut (e.g., post at +515 when best is +510) → high probability of being matched first, BUT only marginal improvement on the existing best

Rules of thumb:

  • 1-2 cents better than current best: safe. You almost always get matched first if a taker arrives. Use this when you suspect a taker is coming soon (close to game time, market is heating up).
  • 5-10 cents better: moderate. You might wait longer or get jumped by another maker. Use when the market is sleepy and you can afford to wait.
  • 20+ cents better: aggressive. Only use if you think the current best price is meaningfully off-market — e.g., you have a model that says fair is way different.
Sliding in on the Burger HR example
Current best Over+510 with $32 available
Conservative post+515 with your stake — almost guaranteed to be matched first if a taker arrives
Moderate post+525 with your stake — small wait, decent improvement
Aggressive post+540 to +560 — bigger improvement, real risk of not getting filled
Pick the level based on your time horizon and conviction. If you're scrolling for opportunities, a conservative +515 keeps you in line; if you have a strong view that Burger goes yard, an aggressive +560 lets the market come to you.

The play, end to end

  1. Scan the exchange for niche markets — player props, long-shot bets, alternate lines.
  2. Find a wide market with implied-prob sum > 102%. The 104%+ examples are gold.
  3. Identify the side you want using the translation rule — read the existing depth, figure out where sharps are positioned. Or just bet a side you have a real opinion on.
  4. Calculate your post price — 1-5 cents better than current best, depending on aggressiveness.
  5. Post your offer at that price with the stake you'd be happy with.
  6. Wait. Either you get matched (great — you got a price the existing market wouldn't have given you), or you don't (cancel and move on to the next opportunity).

That's the entire workflow. No models, no advanced math — just the discipline to not take what's offered and instead post your own better price.

When to post vs. when to take

Posting vs. taking — the basic decision
SituationBest move
Tight market (100-102%), price meaningfully better than soft books Take. Take now, before it moves.
Wide market (102-105%), no rush Post 1-3 cents better than best.
Wide market, urgent (game starting soon) Take the best available. Posting risks no fill.
Very wide market (105%+), early in the day Post aggressively. You have time to find a taker.
Wide market on a side you don't care about Pass. Posting requires conviction or a held opinion.

The simplest heuristic: if the market is wide and you have time, post. If it's tight or urgent, take.

Risks and how to manage them

  • You don't get filled. The market moves, the taker arrives at a different price, or another maker undercuts you with an even better offer. Mitigation: don't post on time-sensitive bets where you have to act before kickoff.
  • The line moves against you. News drops, the favorite gets sharper, your offered price now sits at a soft-side level. Mitigation: cancel and re-post if you can monitor; otherwise, accept the variance — your offer is a limit order, not a market order, and limit orders can become stale.
  • Another maker leapfrogs you. You post +515, someone else posts +518. Now you're behind in line. Mitigation: post a few cents better than you'd like (e.g., +520 when only +515 is needed) to leave headroom for being undercut.
  • Game starts before fill. Most exchanges automatically cancel unfilled offers at game time. You walk away with neither the bet nor the exposure — no harm done, just no edge captured.
  • Big stake, thin book. If you post $2,000 in a market where typical fills are $50, you're advertising yourself as a sharp or whale. Make-and-cancel a few times to build a normal-looking footprint, or split the order across multiple posts.

Why this is a sharp move — not a hack

The maker tactic isn't about exploiting a glitch in the system. It's exactly how stock and crypto traders interact with their own order books. The maker who posts a limit order at a slightly better price than the existing best is the market on that price — by offering, they set the new reference. If a taker arrives, they fill at the new better price.

In sports betting, almost no rec bettors do this. They scroll through the exchange like a sportsbook, take whatever's showing, and move on. That leaves all the maker headroom for everyone else — and the "everyone else" is mostly the same trading firms and syndicates we talked about in Reading Exchange Liquidity.

Most bettors are takers. The sharps are makers. The gap between those two roles, on a wide market, is your edge — if you bother to post instead of click.
