The Vig

Sportsbooks don't charge a commission — they shade every line a few percent in their favor. That hidden shading is the vig, and it's on every bet, every market, every time. Most bettors never see it. The ones who learn to price past it start to win.

What it is

Every line you've ever bet had a tax built in.

It's called the vig (short for vigorish) — also known as the "juice." It's how sportsbooks make money: not by predicting games better than you, but by pricing every line so the odds add up to more than 100%. (You'll also hear the word "hold" used as if it means the same thing — it's close, but not identical. We'll clear that up below.)

Here's the simplest possible example — a coin flip. Each side has a true 50% chance. What would fair odds look like, and what does the book actually post?

Fair Odds
+100 / +100
→
Book Odds
−110 / −110

Bet $100, win $100 either way — that's fair. But the sportsbook posts −110 / −110: you bet $110 to win $100, on either side. That extra $10 you risk per bet is the vig.

Why this actually works (in plain English)

The Airport Currency Exchange

You land in another country with $100 USD. The bank's real exchange rate is 1 USD = 1.30 EUR. But at the airport currency kiosk, two prices are posted:

  • They buy USD at 1.25 — when you sell them your dollars
  • They sell USD at 1.35 — when you buy dollars from them

That 10-cent spread is how the kiosk makes money. Every transaction, in either direction, leaves a small cut for the house. You always pay something just to make the trade.

That's vig. On a real 50/50 bet a "fair" book would offer +100 on both sides — bet $10, win $10. The sportsbook offers −110 on both sides instead: you bet $11 to win $10. That extra dollar per $11 is the sportsbook's currency-kiosk spread. Whichever side wins, the house keeps a cut.

Why this matters: every time you bet at a regular sportsbook, you start in a small hole. To win long-term, your edge has to be big enough to climb out of it — which is why finding +EV prices is the only way to consistently profit. Your edge has to beat the built-in spread.

The math behind it

From Lesson 02 you already know −110 implies a 52.38% chance of winning. Two sides at −110 sum to 104.76% — the math we left hanging at the end of that lesson.

Implied probability of both sides at −110 52.38%  +  52.38%  =  104.76%

That "extra" 4.76% is the vig. It's why two evenly matched teams can both look like a slightly-worse-than-50/50 bet at the same time. The book isn't claiming both teams will win — it's just charging 4.76% on every coin flip.

Vig vs. hold — not the same number

People use "vig" and "hold" as if they're interchangeable. They're close — but they're two different measurements of the same market, and any devig tool will show them as separate numbers.

Vig is the overround — how far the two prices sum past 100%. Our −110 / −110 market summed to 104.76%, so the vig is the 4.76% on top.

Hold is the slice of all the money wagered the book actually keeps over the long run — the vig measured against the full 104.76% that came in, not against 100%.

Same −110 / −110 market, two measurements Vig  = 104.76% − 100%     = 4.76%
Hold = 4.76% ÷ 104.76%  = 4.55%

Think of vig as the sticker markup and hold as the book's actual profit margin. Hold is always a touch lower than vig — a small gap on a −110 market, but it widens on higher-juice lines. So when a tool shows you both, now you know why the two numbers don't match.

One more layer: that 4.55% is the theoretical hold — what the book keeps if money lands evenly on both sides. Books often earn more, because bettors pile onto favorites and the book is happy to let them. But that's a story for a later lesson.

Why it matters more than you think

If you've been winning roughly half your bets at −110 and feeling like you should be break-even, here's why you're not.

You need to win 52.4% of your bets at −110 just to break even. Win exactly 50% and you lose money over time — even though it feels like you're picking right half the time.

Cost of the vig at a 50% win rate — 1,000 bets at −110 500 wins   × +1.00u  =  +500u
500 losses × −1.10u  =  −550u
──────────────────────────
Net result          =  −50u

That's 50 units down over a thousand bets — at $100 a unit, you've quietly handed the books $5,000. You picked half of them right. You still lost. That's the vig, draining your bankroll while you can't see it happening.

What sharps do about it

They strip the vig out of every line. They turn −110 / −110 back into 50% / 50% — the true probability — and only bet when the price they're offered beats that true number, not the vigged one.

Stripping the vig out of a market is called devigging. It's the first move every winning bettor learns. Every other concept on this site — EV, Kelly, CLV — depends on it.

★ Key Point — Beating the Vig Is the Whole Job
Winning at sports betting isn't about picking more winners — picking half of them right at −110 still loses money, as the math above shows. It's about finding prices whose edge is big enough to clear the vig. That's the entire job. Every tool on this site exists to do one of two things: measure the vig (the Devig Calculator) or check whether your edge beats it (the EV Calculator). Master that and you're no longer the customer paying the spread — you're the one shopping for the rare line where the spread tips your way.
