+EV

Expected value is the only kind of bet a winning bettor places. If a wager is +EV, the long run pays you; if it isn't, the long run takes from you. Picks, hot takes, gut calls — all noise. This is the math that replaces them.

What it is

Once you can strip the vig, you know the true probability behind any line. The next question every winning bettor asks: does the price you're being offered beat that probability?

If it does, the bet is +EV — positive expected value. Long-term, you make money on it. If it doesn't, it's −EV, and long-term you lose. There's no middle ground.

Expected value is the average dollar amount you'd make per bet if you placed this exact wager an endless number of times. Most bets at a sportsbook are −EV by design — that's the vig at work. Finding the few that aren't is the entire job.

Why this actually works (in plain English)

The Beach-Day Water Stand

You buy a case of 24 water bottles at Costco for $24 — a dollar each. You walk down to the beach and sell them out of a cooler for $3 apiece.

Some days you sell 5 bottles before someone yells "sketchy" and shoos you off — you're down $19. Some days you sell out in 90 minutes and made $48. Most days land somewhere between.

Over 30 beach days the math is in your favor: you're always pricing above what you paid. The +$2-a-bottle margin doesn't care about any single day. Run it long enough and the wins outpace the losses.

That's +EV betting. Each bet is a beach day — could be good, could be bad. But every bet you place is one where the price hands you a margin over fair value. The edge doesn't show up bet-by-bet. It shows up in the average, once enough bets have let the math work.

In the analogyIn real betting
Buying water at $1Placing a bet at a "+EV price" — better than fair
Selling at $3The payout if the bet wins
A slow beach dayA losing bet, or a losing streak
A busy beach dayA winning bet
30+ beach daysEnough bets for the edge to materialize

The key insight: a +EV bet is +EV the moment you place it, not when it settles. The settling is just variance. You made the right "purchase" the instant you got a better price than the market.

The math behind it

Say a bet's true probability of winning is 55% and you're getting +100 odds (decimal 2.0). Here's what happens on average per $100 risked:

Expected value of a 55% bet at +100 odds Win  (55%):  +$100 × 0.55  =  +$55
Lose (45%):  −$100 × 0.45  =  −$45
────────────────────────────
EV per $100 risked      =  +$10

That's a +10% edge. Place that same bet a thousand times and you'd expect to make about $10,000. You'll never get the identical bet a thousand times — but every +EV bet you find chips away at the books in exactly the same way.

The general formula:

EV percentage EV%  =  ( true probability  ×  decimal odds )  −  1

The catch is in the words true probability. You can't observe it — you estimate it, usually by devigging a sharp market. The EV math is exact; the number you feed it is not. A +EV result is only ever as trustworthy as the fair price behind it.

The friend with the +110 coin flip

The daily coin-flip deal

A friend offers you this: once a day, every day, you flip a fair coin for $10. Heads you win — and because your friend is generous, they pay you at +110. Tails, you lose your $10.

Do you take it? Every single day, forever. It's a 50/50 flip where you win $11 but only lose $10 — a guaranteed +5% edge, every flip:

EV of the friend's flip, per $10 Heads (50%):  +$11 × 0.50  =  +$5.50
Tails  (50%):  −$10 × 0.50  =  −$5.00
────────────────────────────
EV per flip      =  +$0.50

Now here's what actually happens when you play it. Some week, you lose four flips in a row — that's a 1-in-16 shot, so flipping daily it'll likely happen every month or so. You're down $40 on a "sure thing" and it feels like the deal is broken. It isn't. The coin doesn't remember, and the +$0.50 edge is exactly what it was on day one.

  • After 100 flips: you expect to be up ~$50 — but there's still roughly a 1-in-3 chance you're down. That's how loud luck is at small samples.
  • After 1,000 flips: expected profit ~$500, and the chance you're still down has shrunk to about 1 in 15.
  • Keep going and the probability of being behind keeps collapsing toward zero. The edge is quiet, but it never stops pushing.

You'd never cancel the deal because Tuesday through Friday went badly. That's the whole discipline of +EV betting in one sentence.

Sports betting is the same game with one difference: nobody tells you the coin is fair. The book's price claims a probability; your job is to figure out the real one (that's what devigging is for) and only take the flips where the payout beats it. Find the +110-coin-flip deals hiding on the odds board, take every one, and let the sample size do the rest.

Two practical notes that fall straight out of this: size your bets so a 4-loss streak can't knock you out of the game (that's Kelly sizing), and judge yourself on whether you kept finding good flips — not on what the coin did this week.

Why it matters more than you think

Most bettors confuse "I won that bet" with "that was a good bet." They're not the same thing. You can lose a +EV bet — and you will, constantly. You can win a −EV bet and feel like a genius. The result of any single bet tells you almost nothing about whether it was the right bet to make.

EV is signal. Result is noise.

Over thousands of bets, the +EV side always pulls ahead. Over twenty bets, anything can happen — a bettor placing only +EV bets can be down 8 units after a month while a bettor placing only −EV bets is up 12. Over three years, those numbers always invert.

Stop chasing wins and start chasing +EV, and your bankroll's path over the next 1,000 bets looks completely different — even if the next 20 still feel rough.

What sharps do about it

They hunt for +EV obsessively, and pass on everything else.

Most of the work is finding a price somewhere that beats the no-vig fair odds — so it all starts with devigging (Lesson 03). With the true probability in hand, they compare it to the price on offer. If the math says +EV, they bet — even on a side they "don't like." If the math says −EV, they pass — even when the gut screams lock.

The discipline isn't picking winners. It's only making the bets the math approves of, and trusting the long run to sort out the rest.

★ Key Point — EV Is Signal, Result Is Noise
A bet is good or bad the moment you place it — decided entirely by whether the price beat the true probability. Whether it then wins or loses is variance, and variance has no memory. Poker champion Annie Duke calls the opposite habit "resulting" — judging a decision by how it happened to turn out — and it's the single most expensive mistake a new bettor makes. Grade your betting by your process (were the bets +EV?), never by last week's scoreboard. A losing week of all-+EV bets is still a good week; a winning week of −EV bets is a problem in disguise.
