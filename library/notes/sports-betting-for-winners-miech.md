## Thesis in one paragraph

The professional sports-betting world is a small, character-driven ecosystem of disciplined operators — most of whom don't look like the cigar-smoking tough guys of Vegas mythology. The actual playbook of working pros across the city in 2018-19 converges on a tight set of principles: maintain rigorous notebooks; treat your bets like a business, not entertainment; never chase losses with last-game-of-the-day bets; never bet just to have action; bet a fixed unit-as-percentage of bankroll; specialize in a few angles you can stalk for weeks; embrace underdogs (especially home dogs at the right number) and ration favorites; treat the tout industry as 50%+ scum bags; manage to *units won, not bets won*; and stay below the radar (under $10K per bet) to avoid sportsbook profiling, Bank Secrecy Act paperwork, and customer-classification. Sportsbook directors profile every customer into one of eight tiers and play "cat and mouse" with line moves to absorb true sharp action while extracting maximum hold from squares. Miech profiles a dozen operators who exemplify what disciplined sports betting actually looks like — and a critique of the tout industry that surrounds and parasitizes it.

## Key frameworks / named concepts

### 1. The 70-30 (or 80-20) Home-Dog Split — CRITICAL OPERATIONAL TACTIC
A specific tactical rule used by **at least two senior pros** independently (Banker and Youmans), making it the highest-credibility tactical convergence in the book:

- On a strong home-underdog play, allocate **70% of the wager on the points** and **30% on the moneyline.**
- The moneyline portion captures the asymmetric upside if the underdog wins outright.
- **Banker variant:** "Bet the underdog with the points, then put a third of that wager on the moneyline." (Same 70-30 idea expressed slightly differently.)
- **Youmans variant:** when conviction is extreme, escalate to 80-20 (more on points, smaller moneyline kicker). His Purdue +14 over OSU 2018 play used 80-20: four-figure points bet + mid-three-figure moneyline at +500.
- **Underlying logic:** Lem Banker estimates **NFL underdogs win outright 35-40% of the time over many years.** The moneyline payoff (typically +200 or better on a 14-point dog) more than compensates for the lower hit rate.
- Banker's case study: Dallas +3.5 lost by 3 → points covered; moneyline portion was the third he'd allocated, paying +200. Mixed win.

> 🔑 **PPS implication:** This is operational content PPS can ship. A "70-30 Home Dog Calculator" (input bankroll + edge + dog price → output split bet sizes) would be a low-build-cost tool with a real operational use case. Cross-validates and operationalizes MV's FLB reversal at the right circumstances.

### 2. Van Smith's MLB "Bottom-6 vs Top-10" Angle (Ch 3, Blue Suede Shoes)
A specific, replicable MLB betting angle:

- **Trigger:** A bottom-six team (by record) plays a top-ten team. *Skip the first game.*
- **Second game only:** if the top-ten team dropped the opener, bet the home top-ten team to bounce back.
- **Sizing:** Van bets enough to win one "unit" (~2% of bankroll). If the bounce-back fails, fire again in game 3 sized to recoup the loss + the unit profit.
- **Logic:** the price on the home favorite is depressed by the loss-streak narrative (public latches onto streaks) + the favorite has its attention piqued by the embarrassing loss.
- **Van's 2018 result on this single angle:** 16 wins, 0 losses. ~$32,000 net.

> 🔑 **PPS implication:** A specific MLB content piece is shippable — "The Bottom-6 Bounce-Back" — with appropriate "past performance ≠ future results" caveats. Pair with Peta's dime-line + cluster-luck framing for a multi-angle MLB content series.

### 3. The Bail-Out Special / Late-Game Double-Up Trap (Ch 12, Dave Cokin)
**Definition:** Betting the last game of the day (often Hawaii's late-night home football game; also Sunday Night Football, Monday Night Football, Thursday primetime) for the purpose of either *doubling up a winning day* or *getting even after a losing day*.

**Pro consensus:** This is one of the worst betting habits, full stop. Ted Sevransky (Teddy Covers): *"That's a strategy that's doomed to failure. That's ignorant and irresponsible."*

**Why:** the bet isn't being driven by edge — it's driven by emotional state. The action satisfies a psychological need (closure, "get-even-ness," chasing) rather than a probabilistic one.

**Cokin's principle:** "Curtailing losses is a major part of a disciplined approach to betting on sports. … If it's purely for fun, a hobby, with disposable income that will not be missed, partake. But if it's anything more, involving a bankroll and a specific unit of wager, betting on a game just because it's on the big board can have disastrous consequences."

> 🔑 **PPS implication:** Direct cross-reference to Sharper's emotional-discipline content + Duke's tilt-proofing + Peta's "Mental Discomfort of Being Behind" chapter. PPS can publish a "Don't bet the last game" piece with multi-source consensus backing.

### 4. The 8-Tier Customer Profiling System (Ch 1, Jay Kornegay confirming)
**Jay Kornegay (Westgate Las Vegas SuperBook VP):** "We monitor and classify each of our customers in eight distinctions, from the sharpest of the sharp to your average Joe."

This explicitly confirms and extends LOSB's 1-5 sharpness scale. The Westgate uses **eight tiers** of customer profiling. Operationally:
- **Sharp action** is identified and used as informational input (line moves)
- **Mid-tier action** is leaned into for hold extraction
- **Square action** is the bread and butter

**The cat-and-mouse:** Kornegay explicitly describes detecting "false moves" (where a sharp bets early to disguise the side they actually want) vs "true moves" — and adjusting lines accordingly. **A two-source confirmation** (LOSB + Miech-via-Kornegay) that customer profiling at Vegas sportsbooks is a structured, sophisticated practice.

### 5. The $10K Bank Secrecy Act Rule (Bank Secrecy Act 1985, Rule 6A)
Operational compliance fact most PPS readers don't know:

- Any wager of $10,000 or more triggers a Currency Transaction Report (CTR) to the Nevada Gaming Control Board, which notifies the IRS.
- Pros like Van Smith deliberately stay below $10K per bet to operate "below the radar."
- Roxborough: *"If you're not betting enough to move the lines, people can live under the radar for a long, long time."*

This is the operational reason serious bettors split larger positions into multiple smaller bets, and one of the silent reasons sportsbooks have an easier time identifying high-rollers. **No other source in our library names this rule explicitly.**

### 7. The "Stalk Before You Bet" Approach (Ch 7, Matt Youmans)
Youmans's signature method:
- Identify a potential bet *weeks* before pulling the trigger
- Watch every game involving both teams in the interim
- Track every variable that would confirm or invalidate the thesis
- Only act when *every single sign* aligns with the original read

Worked example: Youmans stalked Purdue +14 over OSU for **a month** in 2018, watching Ohio State fail to cover 4 of 5 games + injury reports on Nick Bosa + Purdue's 4-game cover streak + Brohm's aggressive playcalling. When every signal converged, he escalated his standard 70-30 split to 80-20 — and Purdue won outright 49-20.

**Cross-pollination:** This is the operational embodiment of Duke's "I'm not sure" framework + Sharper's "estimated edge is not edge" + Peta's variable bet sizing. The pro doesn't bet many games — but when they bet, they've done the work.

### 8. The Tout/Scamdicapper Critique (Ch 12, Brussels Sprouts)
Multi-pro consensus on the tout industry. Key red flags:

- **"Ten-Star Picks" / "Five-Unit Plays" / "Twenty-Star Diamond-Jubilee"** terminology
- **Recommending wagers >2-3% of bankroll on a single game** — Cokin: "That is so fucked up. You cannot win doing that."
- **The split-pitch scam:** "selling" Team A to half the clientele and Team B to the other half; whichever wins becomes the next month's "proof" call
- **Selling picks for hundreds/thousands per week** vs. transparent flat monthly fees
- **Hidden track records** vs. publicly registered plays on independent monitors

**Stewart's framing:** "Scum bags. There's a way around it in this industry, to not be that scum-bag guy. I'd say more than half are scum-bag guys."

**Barton's counter-model:** $99/month flat fee, no escalation tiers, no upsells, every play registered publicly on independent monitors, no call centers (email only), transparent record including losses.

**Kelly Stewart's tout cycle insight:** "When you're winning, selling picks is great. But when you're losing, selling picks makes it worse because not only are you losing, yourself, but you're losing for your clients. … You know the regression is coming, you just don't know when."

> 🔑 **PPS implication:** Direct content angle for the brand's anti-tout positioning. "How to spot a sports-betting tout vs an honest handicapper" — checklist guide. Brand-aligned, contrarian, shareable.

### 9. The 1-3% Per Bet Sizing Convergence (Ch 12, Dave Cokin)
**Cokin's standard:** "I have a bankroll, separate from my living expenses, and my normal wager is between one and three percent."

Convergence with our library:
- **Peta** (Trading Bases): 10bp-2% tiered (most bets sub-1%)
- **Sharper:** explicit fractional-Kelly humility
- **Mathletics:** Kelly says don't exceed optimal-f; at 60% winners that's ~14.5% but warns against
- **Duke:** size-based-on-confidence
- **Miech via Cokin:** 1-3% range as the working-pro standard

**The working-pro consensus is ~1-3% of bankroll per bet, with rare conviction plays up to 5%.** Anyone selling "10-unit plays" (which typically means 5-10% of bankroll) is incompatible with this consensus.

### 10. "American Public Roots for the Underdog But Bets Favorites" (Michael Gaughan, South Point owner)
A direct quote-on-record from one of Vegas's most senior sportsbook owners. This is a **third-source confirmation** of:
- **Levitt 2004** (squares bet favorites in NFL spreads; via Mathletics)
- **Scorecasting** (loss-aversion drives favorite bias)
- **MV** (lottery preference is on underdog moneylines specifically)

Gaughan's framing reconciles the apparent paradox: emotionally, the public *roots* for the underdog (because underdog stories are romantic), but financially, they *bet* favorites (because favorites feel safer). This is consistent with PPS Synthesis #1 (Favorite-bias × Longshot-bias coexist) — favorites on the spread, longshots on the moneyline, both at different decision moments.

> 🔑 **Quote-worthy:** Gaughan's exact words, on the record from one of Vegas's senior sportsbook owners. Strong citation for any PPS content explaining the favorite-bias mechanism.

### 11. The 52.38% / 57-58% / 54-55% Pro Bettor Hit-Rate Reality
Multi-source synthesis from the book:
- **UNLV International Gaming Institute** estimate: "**at best, 3% of bettors can win consistently at a rate of 57-58%**"
- **Paul Stone (pro handicapper):** "Hit fifty-four to fifty-five percent in this business, over a period of time, and you're big time."
- **Standard break-even at -110:** 52.38%

**The actual sustainable pro hit-rate range is 54-58%.** Anyone claiming 60%+ over significant sample is almost certainly:
- A tout claiming false numbers
- A short-term streaker who hasn't reverted yet
- Cherry-picking the games they include

Cross-references with **Sharper's "anyone claiming 60% needs to sell their picks for a reason"** and **Peta's 51.9% hit-rate-with-discipline producing +41% return**.

### 12. The "You Never, Ever Know" Mantra (Jimmy Vaccaro)
From Vaccaro, one of Vegas's most senior oddsmakers, repeated throughout the book: *"You never, ever know when it comes to athletes and sports."*

Operational implication: any system or model that claims certainty is wrong. Variance is irreducible. Even 58%-hit-rate pros lose 42% of the time.

Cross-pollination with **Duke's "I'm not sure"** + **Sharper's "estimated edge is not edge"** + **Peta's "Markets can remain irrational longer than you can remain solvent"**. Four sources, one principle: calibrated uncertainty as professional discipline.

### 13. The Pro-Bettor Lifestyle Reality Check
Miech profiles bettors who actually make a living at this and contrasts them with the popular fantasy:

- **Van Smith:** rents a one-bedroom condo, no house, drives a leased Cadillac, makes ~$200K/yr betting + retirement income
- **Lem Banker:** 91 years old at time of writing, ailing, formerly the Godfather of Sports Betting, now limited to four offshore accounts, $1,000 max bets
- **Matt Youmans:** comfortable condo, salary at VSiN + selective bets, "you don't want to work at a blue-collar nine-to-five the rest of your life"
- **Kelly Stewart:** parlay tickets are $100; the dream is *not* the YOLO action — it's the consistent edge

**Counter to the Hollywood version:** pro bettors are disciplined people with notebooks, schedules, and steady habits. Not high-rollers in casino loft suites. Cross-references **Sharper Ch 31 ("The Life")** + **Peta's drawdown reporting**.

## Pedagogical patterns

Miech's structure offers a few PPS-borrowable moves but a number of patterns to avoid:

### Effective patterns to borrow
- **Character-per-chapter framing.** Each chapter centers a different practitioner. Variety keeps engagement. PPS can borrow for an "Operators" content series — each piece profiles a different real-world bettor with their methodology + a Pull Quote + a Reader Action item.
- **The "Skinny" callout boxes.** Miech embeds a short, scannable summary box ("THE SKINNY") in most chapters, distilling the practical takeaway. PPS can use callout boxes the same way for in-line lesson summaries.
- **Cross-confirming the same principle from multiple practitioners.** Miech often returns to the same idea from a different pro's voice. PPS can replicate: stating a principle then citing 2-3 separate operators who use it.
- **The Vegas-color voice.** Miech's writing is sensory — the colors, the smells, the lighting. Used sparingly, this kind of texture can ease the math content for a reader. PPS already does some of this; Miech confirms the approach.
- **Naming free third-party resources.** When Stewart names Kenny White et al., the reader knows where to go next. PPS should be more aggressive about citing free resources by name.

### What we'd avoid
- **Profile-heavy, framework-light overall structure.** PPS content needs higher operational density than Miech's book.
- **Vegas-mythology drift.** Some chapters drift into Lefty Rosenthal / mob / boxing history that's entertaining but off-topic. PPS stays operational.
- **Celebrity-anecdote interludes.** Pete Rose, Jimmie Walker, Steve Lawrence. Color, not content.
- **The melancholy elegy treatment** (Banker chapter). PPS doesn't profile struggling pros without a clear lesson takeaway.
- **2018-19-specific personnel + apps name-dropping** that will date quickly. PPS uses evergreen language with citations updated periodically.
