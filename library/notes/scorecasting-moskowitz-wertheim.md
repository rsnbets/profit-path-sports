## Thesis in one paragraph

Sports are governed by hidden behavioral and economic forces. Officials are accurate but biased — overwhelmingly toward home teams, especially in high-leverage moments and in calls with maximum discretion (loose-ball fouls, traveling, holding penalties, called strikes on the corners). Home-field advantage is real and constant across all sports and eras, but NONE of the conventional explanations (crowd noise, travel rigors, roster construction, weather) actually hold up to data — when you isolate each variable, the home-field effect vanishes. What's left is referee bias, and it accounts for the majority of HFA in every sport with discretionary officiating (~2/3 in MLB called pitches, ~3/4 in NBA fouls, ~80% in NHL penalties, ~half in NCAA via schedule padding + refs). Coaches systematically make decisions that reduce their team's chances of winning — punting on 4th down when they shouldn't, kicking field goals when they should go for it — because they're driven by loss aversion (Kahneman-Tversky 1979) and fear of being blamed for visible decisions rather than invisible ones. The "hot hand" doesn't exist (Gilovich, Vallone & Tversky 1985 confirmed across two decades of further research). Icing the kicker doesn't work. Selective statistics ("4 of his last 5") almost always mean "4 of his last 6+." We confuse the recognizable for the causal and credit/blame the wrong forces for the outcomes we see.

## Key frameworks / named concepts

### 1. Omission Bias (Ch: Whistle Swallowing) — CRITICAL CONCEPT
- People view *acts of omission* as less intrusive/harmful than *acts of commission* — even when outcomes are the same or worse.
- **Vaccination study:** 10/10,000 die from flu unvaccinated; 5/10,000 die from vaccine. Most parents won't vaccinate — they'd "feel responsible" for vaccine-caused death but not for non-vaccination death.
- **Tennis-allergy study:** subjects rate "recommending the allergenic food to opponent" as far more immoral than "not warning opponent who orders it themselves."
- **NBA referee training** explicitly categorizes calls as: correct calls, incorrect calls, correct noncalls, **incorrect noncalls**. The unwritten directive: "When the game steps up, you step down." Get an incorrect call wrong and you're in deep trouble. Get an incorrect noncall wrong and you're fine.
- **Jeff Bezos:** "People overfocus on errors of commission. Companies overemphasize how expensive failure's going to be. The big cost most companies incur is much harder to notice — errors of omission."

**For PPS:** Omission bias maps directly onto bettor behavior. Bettors regret bets they place and lose more than equivalent bets they didn't place and would have won. This is the cognitive mechanism behind "I should've trusted my gut" regret. Combine with Duke's *resulting* for a complete pain-point lesson.

### 2. The 4th-Down / Loss Aversion Framework (Ch: Go For It)
**Romer 2005 (NBER paper):** Based on data from 700+ NFL games and 1,068 fourth-down situations where math says go-for-it, **NFL coaches punted 959 times (~90%).**

**Operating rules from Romer:**
- Inside opponent's 45 yard line, anything less than 4th-and-8: go for it.
- Inside opponent's 33: anything less than 4th-and-11: go for it.
- **Regardless of field position, anything less than 4th-and-5: go for it.**

**Kevin Kelley case study (Pulaski Academy, Arkansas HS):** Never punts. Onside kicks routinely. Doesn't return punts. Record: 77-17-1, 3 state title appearances, 2 wins. Math:
- Punting from deep gives opponent the ball ~40-yard line → 77% TD rate
- Going for it on 4th-and-long and failing inside the 10 → 92% TD rate
- **Only 15% increase in opponent TD chance for the risk of keeping possession**

**Why coaches don't change:** **Loss aversion** (Kahneman & Tversky 1979). "Losing hurts twice as much as winning feels good."
- The mutual-fund-manager analogy: hold Walmart, lose with the crowd, don't get fired. Hold an obscure pick, lose, get blamed and fired.
- Coaches' incentives are misaligned with team's actual best interest: 8-8 keeping your job > 9-7 being labeled reckless.

**For PPS:** This is the operational explanation for *why* the betting public is biased toward favorites (Levitt 2004) and why book lines stay inflated. Loss aversion infects coaches, bettors, executives, and fans alike. **Cross-pollinates with everything in our library.**

### 3. Home-Field Advantage: The Mechanism (Ch: Comforts of Home + So What Is Driving HFA) — ⭐⭐⭐ FLAGSHIP CONTENT

**The cross-sport HFA empirical table** (cross-validates and extends Mathletics's σ values):

| League | Home win % |
|---|---|
| MLB | 54% |
| NBA | 62.7% |
| NFL | ~58% |
| NHL | 59% |
| NCAA basketball | 69% |
| NCAA football | 64% |
| International soccer (43 leagues) | 62.4% |
| International rugby | 58% |
| International cricket | 60% |

**The four conventional explanations — DEBUNKED:**

1. **Crowd noise doesn't matter.** NBA free throw % is identical at home (75.9%) and away (75.9%). NHL shootout success: home 49.4%, away 50.6%. NFL kicker accuracy from same distance: identical. MLB pitcher velocity/movement/location: identical at home and away via Pitch f/x. **Even free-throw shooters DON'T choke in front of hostile crowds.**

2. **Travel rigors don't matter.** "Same city" games (Lakers vs Clippers, Yankees vs Mets) show the exact same HFA. International border crossings (US-Canada in NHL) show no abnormal HFA. MLB HFA was the same in 1903-1909 as in 2003-2009 — modern travel improvements didn't change anything.

3. **Roster construction for home features doesn't matter.** "Hitters' parks" don't outhit visitors more than "pitchers' parks." Cold-weather NFL teams don't disproportionately win at home in cold conditions. Dome teams (placebo) show same HFA patterns.

4. **Scheduling bias** — *partial truth.* NBA back-to-backs slightly disfavor road teams (explains ~21% of NBA HFA). College sports schedule "patsy" opponents at home (explains ~50% of NCAAF/NCAAB HFA). In MLB and NFL, scheduling explains essentially zero.

**THE REAL ANSWER: Referee bias.** Confirmed across every sport with discretionary officiating:

**Soccer (Garicano, Palacios-Huerta & Prendergast):** Refs add extra "injury time" when home team is behind, shorten it when home team is ahead. La Liga sample: 4 min extra when home down by 1, 2 min when home up by 1. Bias eliminates when score is lopsided (no benefit to ref reputation). Replicated in EPL, Serie A, Bundesliga, Scottish league, MLS. **Yellow/red cards** also biased: a yellow + red card given to a visiting player jumps home win probability to 59%.

**MLB:** Home batters get **far fewer called strikes per called pitch** than away batters. The effect grows with **Leverage Index** (Tom Tango's metric) — in low-leverage situations the bias vanishes; in high-leverage situations it's enormous. **QuesTec natural experiment:** in stadiums with the umpire-monitoring camera system, the home-team strike-call advantage didn't just disappear — it *flipped against the home team*, because umpires overcorrected. Same umpires, same season, opposite calls based on whether they're being watched. Total impact: **~7.3 extra runs per season per home team from plate umpire bias alone. Average MLB HFA in run differential is 10.5 runs/season → umpire bias accounts for ~2/3 of MLB HFA.**

**NFL:** Home teams get half a penalty fewer per game; more first-down penalties go home team's way. **Instant replay natural experiment:** introduced in 1999. Home team success rate dropped from 58.5% (1985-1998) to 56% (1999-2008) — a 29.4% drop in HFA. Pre-instant-replay, home teams had an 8% turnover edge — cut in half after instant replay. Fumble rates are identical home/away; **fumble-recovery rates were biased** before replay let visitors challenge.

**NBA:** Same-distance free throws identical. But home teams shoot 1-1.5 more FT per game because **away teams are called for more fouls** — and specifically the *ambiguous* fouls (loose-ball, traveling, offensive fouls) that require ref judgment. Unambiguous violations (shot clock, 5-second inbounds) show NO bias. Change-of-possession fouls are 4× more likely to go home team's way. **Total ref-bias contribution: ~75% of NBA HFA (3.4 pts/game).**

**NHL:** Home teams get 20% fewer penalties + less severe penalties. **2.5 more minutes of power play per game.** At ~20% goal conversion rate per power play, that's 0.25 goals/game. Average NHL HFA is 0.30 goals/game → ref bias accounts for **~83% of NHL HFA.** Shootouts (no ref discretion) show no HFA. Ambiguous penalties (holding, hooking, cross-checking, boarding, tripping) show large bias; unambiguous (too many men, illegal equipment) show none.

**WHY are refs biased?** Not corruption. **Conformity / social pressure** (Muzafer Sherif 1935). Refs are subject to crowd influence the same way participants in conformity experiments adjust their estimates toward the group. **The bigger the crowd, the worse the bias** (empirically demonstrated in NHL by comparing penalty differentials by attendance).

PPS can ship "The Real Reason Home Teams Win" as a flagship piece combining Mathletics's σ values + Sharper's HFA compression + Scorecasting's mechanism + Levitt's book-exploitation framework. **Cross-source synthesis-grade content.**

### 4. The Hot Hand Is a Myth (Ch: Myth of the Hot Hand) — CROSS-VALIDATES MATHLETICS
- **Gilovich, Vallone & Tversky 1985** (the canonical paper): tracked 76ers field goals + Celtics free throws. **No evidence of momentum.** Success on previous shot does not predict success on next shot.
- **Cornell varsity experiment:** controlled environment, no defense/crowd/pressure. Same result: no hot hand.
- **Surveys:** 91% of fans believed in the hot hand. The players themselves believed it. **But the data doesn't.**
- **Huizinga & Weil follow-up (2002-2008 NBA):** Same conclusion, plus a behavioral finding: **shooters who "feel hot" take harder shots and shoot 3.5 pts below their normal FG%.** "Feeding the hot hand" costs teams ~4.5 wins/season on average.
- **NBA team-level momentum** (Scorecasting's own analysis): teams with 6+ unanswered points in the prior minute were *outscored by 0.31 points* in the next minute. **Reversal, not momentum.**
- **Postseason "momentum heading in":** 10-game win streak entering the playoffs offers no advantage over a 10-game losing streak.
- Hot-hand effects DO exist in two sports: **bowling and billiards** — repetitive motion, same physical space. That's the boundary condition.

**For PPS:** This is the definitive academic refutation chain (Gilovich/Vallone/Tversky 1985 → Huizinga & Weil → Scorecasting). Cross-validates Mathletics Ch 11 (Albright on MLB streak hitting, Mathletics's own 2002-3 NBA z-test analysis). **Triple-source consensus** that hot hand doesn't exist — PPS can ship this with maximum confidence.

### 5. The Gambler's Fallacy + Lottery Number Pattern (Ch: Myth of the Hot Hand)
- Most people think 10 heads in a row → next flip more likely to be tails. **It's 50%.** No "law of averages" exists.
- **State lottery insight:** since most lottery jackpots are split among winners, you maximize your *expected per-winner payoff* by picking *repetitive* digits (22222) that nobody else picks. The lottery is random — 22222 is as likely as 65821 — but humans avoid it because it "doesn't look random."
- **Alphonse Chapanis 1953:** asked subjects to write "random" digit sequences. Almost no one used 222 or 333. Subjects can't generate random sequences.
- **Casino practice:** posting recent roulette results exploits gambler's fallacy — bettors think they're "due" for opposite outcomes.

**For PPS:** Direct content angle. Sports bettors believe in "due-ness" — a team "due to cover after losing 4 straight," a kicker "due to miss after 8 makes." The math says no. This connects to Sharper's "system unattached to ratings" critique and Duke's "resulting."

### 6. Damned Statistics / Selective Framing (Ch: Damned Statistics)
- **Heuristic:** "A player has reached base in 4 of his last 5 at-bats" almost always means 4 of his last 6. Otherwise the broadcaster would say 5 of 6.
- "Lost 3 in a row" → usually 3 of last 4, 5, or 6. Otherwise it'd be "lost 4 in a row."
- **The A-Rod vs Halladay framing example:** the same true facts produce opposite expectations depending on what subset of stats the broadcaster picks.
- **Ray Allen 2010 finals:** Game 2 made 8 of 11 threes, Game 3 missed all 13. Media coverage cherry-picked both ways. **Combined across both games:** 8 of 19 (42%), exactly his career average.

**For PPS:** This is the operational complement to Duke's "decision swear jar." Any time betting media gives a stat with "last 5" or "last 10" or "in this situation since 2018," ask: what's the larger sample? Why this slice? Cross-pollinates with Sharper's "in the line" critique (don't bet on info already absorbed) and Duke's belief-vetting.

### 7. Icing the Kicker Doesn't Work (Ch: Do Athletes Really Melt When Iced)
- Scorecasting's NFL 2001-2009 study: **iced kickers are successful at the same rate as non-iced kickers** from the same distance.
- Berry & Wood (Chance journal): some evidence on 40-55 yard pressure kicks (10% drop) but small sample.
- Nick Stamm (STATS Inc.) 1991-2004: 72% iced vs 71.7% non-iced — statistically no different.
- NBA free throws in pressure moments (last 2 min, within 5 points): iced 76%, non-iced 76%. Identical.
- **Sometimes backfires** (Jay Feely 2008 Jets/Raiders): "free dress rehearsal" before the real kick.

**For PPS:** Operational betting content. Don't bet against an iced kicker. Don't bet against an iced free-throw shooter in clutch moments. The bias against them in market prop pricing may even be exploitable.

### 8. Self-Attribution Bias (Ch: Are the Chicago Cubs Cursed)
- People credit success to skill and failure to luck.
- We don't call the Yankees "lucky" (1 in 32 billion outcome of 27 titles in 100 years) but we call the Cubs "cursed" (3.5% chance of a 100-year drought given 30 teams = 1 in 30, not extraordinary).
- Direct overlap with Duke's *fielding outcomes* — same bias, different label.

**For PPS:** Reinforcement of Duke's framework with a different cultural anchor (Cubs/Yankees). The Cubs-curse-as-self-attribution lesson is a great mass-market entry point to probabilistic thinking.

### 9. Going-First in NFL Overtime (Ch: How a Coin Toss Trumps All)
- Pre-2010 NFL OT rules: ~60% of OT games were won by the team that won the coin toss and got the ball first.
- The rule was structurally biased. (Rules changed partially in 2010 and significantly in 2017.)
- Useful as historical case study of structurally unfair rules.

### 10. NFL Draft Pick Valuation (Ch: Off the Chart)
- NFL coaches and GMs use the "Jimmy Johnson Draft Value Chart" (1989) — pick #1 worth 3,000 points, pick #2 worth 2,600, etc.
- **Massimo Massa & Cade Massey research:** the chart vastly overvalues high picks. The marginal value of a pick #1 over pick #11 is far smaller than the chart implies. **Trade math suggests teams should trade DOWN, not UP, in the draft.**
- Direct connection to *MV's FLB framework*: bettors and GMs both overweight rare big-win possibilities. Lottery preferences in human resource decisions.

**For PPS:** Bridges to a draft-betting / futures content angle (player props, win-totals, ROY odds, etc.).

- Same umpire, same season

- **Umpire behavior flipped 180° based purely on whether they knew they were being watched.**

## Weak claims / limitations / criticisms

- **Mass-market voice over technical depth.** Almost every claim has academic backing, but the writing softens the math. PPS readers wanting rigor will need the cited primary sources (Romer 2005, Gilovich-Vallone-Tversky 1985, etc.).
- **Some chapters drift into culture/commentary** with limited PPS application (Tiger Woods, Cubs-as-cursed cultural history, "There's No I In Team," "Thanks Mr. Rooney" on NFL black coaches, NFL draft).
- **Soccer references throughout.** Per user constraint, PPS doesn't cover soccer. But the *mechanism* findings (injury-time bias, yellow-card bias) generalize to "discretionary calls favor home teams" which applies to every sport. Use the soccer data as supporting evidence, not as content focus.
- **2011 publication date.** Some of the specific examples (Tim Donaghy, Bartman) are now culturally aged. The empirical findings remain valid.
- **No engagement with sportsbook market structure.** Scorecasting talks about what happens *on the field*; LOSB/Funt/Sharper/Mathletics talk about what happens *at the book*. Scorecasting doesn't engage with line-setting, hold, or bettor behavior at the sportsbook level. **Limitation that PPS naturally fills.**
- **No betting-action implications drawn.** Scorecasting tells you the data but doesn't connect it to "how should you bet?" PPS makes that connection.
- **One specific cross-source tension worth flagging:** Scorecasting confirms the HFA-mechanism story but doesn't engage with how this interacts with the *line-setting* process. The book lines already account for HFA (Mathletics: 3 pts NFL, etc.). The question for bettors isn't "does HFA exist" but "is the market accurately pricing HFA after the ref-bias finding became known?" — a question Scorecasting doesn't address.
