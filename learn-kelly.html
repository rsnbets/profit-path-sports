Kelly Sizing

Picking the right side is only half the bet. How much to stake is the other half — and that's exactly what Kelly solves. Size by gut and even a real edge goes broke. Here's how to do it right.

What is it? — the coin-flip thought experiment

A friend offers you a bet on a coin flip. Fair coin — 50/50 — but he's paying +110. You win, you get $1.10 for every $1 you bet. You lose, you lose your $1.

First — is this even a good bet? Yes. Win or lose is 50/50, but the payouts aren't — you win $1.10 yet only risk $1.00. Averaged across both outcomes, every dollar you bet comes back with a small profit. That average is the bet's expected value (EV), and a positive EV is what makes any bet worth taking — it's the whole subject of Lesson 04, if it's new to you. Here's the math:

Expected value per $1 bet 0.5 × $1.10 − 0.5 × $1.00  =  +$0.05

Five cents of profit on every dollar — a 5% edge, well worth taking. But that only settles whether to bet. How much to bet is the harder question — and the one that can wipe you out even with the edge in your favor. (Spoiler: betting your whole bankroll is never the answer.)

Now your friend says: same bet, but unlimited flips, on a fixed bankroll. How much should you bet on each flip?

There has to be an optimal amount. The two extremes are obviously wrong:

  • Bet 100% of your bankroll every flip → you'll lose roughly half the time → the first loss busts you out and you can never bet again. A 5% edge ruined you. Long-run growth: zero, permanently.
  • Bet 0% → no growth at all.

Somewhere between 0% and 100% is the bet size that maximizes the long-run growth rate of your bankroll. That sweet spot is what the Kelly Criterion calculates. It answers the math problem: given my edge and my odds, what fraction of my bankroll should I bet to grow it fastest without going broke?

For the +110 coin flip, the answer is ~4.55% of your current bankroll on each flip. A $1,000 bankroll = $45.45 per flip. Win, the next bet is 4.55% of the bigger stack. Lose, the next bet is 4.55% of the smaller one. Bet size always scales with bankroll, so a single loss is never fatal.

It's the same formula professional bettors, traders, and quant funds use to size positions. Not a rule of thumb — an answer to a math problem.

Why this actually works (in plain English)

The Sister's Side Hustle

Your sister opens a food truck. Over Sunday dinner she tells you: "I think I've got a 70% chance of being profitable in year one. If I am, you'd double your money in 18 months. If I'm not, you lose everything you put in."

Sounds like a real edge. But the question isn't whether to back her — it's how much.

  • All your savings ($30K) → if it fails, you can't pay rent. Bad call even though you're 70% confident.
  • $50 just to be polite → if she crushes it, you barely benefit. Wasted the edge.
  • Somewhere in between → that's Kelly. A formula that says: given your edge (70% with a 2× payoff is real) and your bankroll, here's the optimal percentage to risk.

In her case, full Kelly says you'd put in about 40% of your savings. Most people would use quarter-Kelly (10%) — same direction, smaller risk, sleep better.

The takeaway: there's a mathematically optimal amount to bet on any edge. Bet too much, ruin is inevitable. Bet too little, you leave growth on the table. Kelly tells you the right ballpark.

The math behind it

Underneath, Kelly is simple: bet a slice of your bankroll proportional to how big your edge is, relative to the payout. Two inputs go in — your edge and the odds — one number comes out: the fraction to stake.

Kelly % = edge payout
edge  =  (decimal odds × true probability) − 1 Your expected value on each $1 staked — how much the bet is worth.
payout  =  decimal odds − 1 The profit on each $1 if the bet wins.

Run it on two bets — the coin flip from earlier, and a realistic sports bet. Same three steps each time:

Coin Flip
+110 odds (decimal 2.10) · 50% true probability
edge= 2.10 × 0.50 − 1 = 0.05
payout= 2.10 − 1 = 1.10
Kelly= 0.05 ÷ 1.10 = 4.55%
Sports Bet
+100 odds (decimal 2.0) · 55% true probability
edge= 2.0 × 0.55 − 1 = 0.10
payout= 2.0 − 1 = 1.0
Kelly= 0.10 ÷ 1.0 = 10%

On a $1,000 bankroll, the sports bet's 10% Kelly is a $100 stake. The bigger your edge relative to the payout, the bigger the Kelly fraction — that's the whole logic.

The peak — and the cliff just past it

The most counterintuitive part of Kelly: you can lose money long-term placing only +EV bets if you size them wrong.

The growth curve has a peak at the Kelly fraction. To the left of the peak (smaller bets) you grow slower. To the right of the peak you don't just grow slower — you fall off a cliff.

Long-run bankroll growth vs. bet size, for the +110 coin flip. The cyan dots mark ¼, ½, and ¾ Kelly climbing the slope — notice how the curve flattens near the top: half Kelly already captures roughly 75% of peak growth at half the bet size and far less variance. Past the peak it falls — zero growth at 2× Kelly, and beyond that you lose money on a bet that's still +EV. Bigger is only better up to the peak.

That's not a typo. Triple Kelly on a +EV bet loses money over time. The reason: bankrolls grow geometrically, not arithmetically. A 50% loss requires a 100% gain to recover. The bigger the bet size, the deeper the drawdowns — and at some point the drawdowns swamp the wins no matter how much edge you have.

Kelly finds the peak. Past the peak, the math turns on you fast.

Why it matters more than you think

Full Kelly is mathematically optimal for long-run growth — but it's brutally volatile. Even with a real edge, a normal bad month at full Kelly can cut your bankroll in half. That's not a tail risk. That's just variance.

So most winning bettors (the industry calls them 'sharps') run fractional Kelly — half-Kelly or quarter-Kelly. You give up a slice of growth in exchange for far less variance and a much smaller chance of ruin. Half-Kelly keeps roughly 75% of full-Kelly's long-run growth with about half the swings; quarter-Kelly, roughly 44% of the growth with about a quarter the swings — and the calmest ride through a losing streak.

Roughly speaking — for a real edge Full Kelly      → max growth, max swings (50%+ drawdowns common)
Half Kelly      → ~75% of the growth, ~half the swings
Quarter Kelly   → ~44% of the growth, ~a quarter the swings

On the sports-bet example above — 10% full Kelly — that's a 5% bet at half-Kelly, 2.5% at quarter. If you've ever blown up a bankroll on a "lock" you sized way too big, there's no version of Kelly that says to do that. There is no "go all-in" in Kelly's book.

What winning bettors do about it

They never bet flat. A 1% edge gets a small bet. A 5% edge gets a bigger one. A 10% edge gets the full Kelly fraction. Most run quarter- or half-Kelly to stay in the game when (not if) variance hits — because the edge they think they have is always a guess, and over-betting an over-estimated edge is how careers end.

The discipline isn't bigger bets when you're confident. It's right-sized bets, every time, regardless of how it feels.

★ Key Point — Full Kelly Lies to You
Every Kelly number is only as good as the win probability you fed it — and that number is an estimate, not a fact. Bet full Kelly on an edge that's even slightly over-stated and you're over-betting, which drains a bankroll even when the edge is real. The half- or quarter-Kelly haircut isn't caution for its own sake — it's the margin that covers the error in your own estimate. When in doubt, bet less.
