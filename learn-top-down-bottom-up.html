Concept · Betting Style

Top-Down vs. Bottom-Up

Two completely different routes to +EV. One trusts the market and shops the cracks. The other builds its own number and bets against the market. Both work — for opposite reasons. Knowing which one you're doing is the first step to actually doing it well.

The two routes

Every profitable sports bettor is doing one of two things — or some blend of the two:

Top-Down

  • Start from a sharp benchmark (Pinnacle, Circa, no-vig market consensus).
  • Treat that benchmark as "fair" — the market is your model.
  • Shop other books for prices that disagree with the benchmark.
  • Bet whichever side is mispriced.
  • You don't need an opinion on the game.

Bottom-Up

  • Build your own projection for a player, team, or game.
  • Convert that projection into a fair price.
  • Compare your fair price to market prices.
  • Bet wherever the market disagrees with you.
  • You're betting against market consensus, not with it.

Both can be +EV. Both can be very profitable. They live or die on completely different things.

How top-down works

Top-down rests on one assumption: the sharp market is approximately right.

That's a defensible assumption for big-volume markets — NFL spreads, NBA totals, MLB moneylines, EPL match odds. Pinnacle and Circa take huge action from sharp bettors and adjust quickly. Their no-vig price is, on average, the best estimate of the true probability available anywhere.

If you trust that benchmark, the rest is mechanical:

  • Take the sharp price (or no-vig consensus across 5+ tight books).
  • Convert to fair probability via an odds calculator.
  • Scan softer books for any line that prices the same outcome at a worse implied probability.
  • Bet the gap.
Top-down example — NFL Sunday total
PinnacleOver 47.5 at −110no-vig 50.0% true
DraftKingsOver 47.5 at −10851.9% implied — fair
Hard RockOver 47.5 at +10548.8% implied
Hard Rock's price implies the market is wrong about this total. Top-down says: trust the market, bet Hard Rock's Over. ~2.4% edge.

You didn't watch a single snap. You didn't read an injury report. You didn't pull a single advanced stat. The bet is profitable as long as Pinnacle is closer to truth than Hard Rock — and on volume, Pinnacle wins that battle.

This is what most modern +EV tools (OddsJam, Unabated, Betstamp, etc.) do. They're top-down engines — surfacing prices that disagree with consensus.

How bottom-up works

Bottom-up flips the assumption: the market is wrong, and I know why.

That's a much harder claim. To make it credibly, you need to build a model — formal or informal — that produces a number for the prop or game. Then you bet wherever the market price disagrees with your number.

Examples:

  • Player props. You build a hits projection for an MLB hitter using batter-vs-pitcher matchup, ballpark, weather, recent form. Your model says he's 78% to record a hit. Market says 70%. You bet his Over 0.5 hits.
  • Strikeout props. Pitcher Ks are notoriously model-friendly — opposing lineup K rate, plate-discipline data, expected pitch count, ump zone. A solid K projection model produces 5%+ edges routinely on lower-volume markets.
  • NCAA / niche sports. College basketball and football, lower-tier soccer, NHL props — markets where Pinnacle isn't booking and the sharp consensus is thin. Your model can be the sharp number.
  • Live betting. In-game models that price the next-drive moneyline, next-half total, etc. — books often lag obvious context (red-zone position, possession arrow) by several seconds, and your model exploits the lag.
Bottom-up example — pitcher strikeout prop
Your modelCole projects 6.4 Ks tonightP(7+ Ks) = 42%
MarketCole Over 6.5 Ks at −14058% implied
Your model says Under 6.5 is +EV. Market says you're wrong. Bet anyway — that's bottom-up.

The math is the same shape (compare your fair to the market and bet the gap). The risk is completely different. If your model is broken, every bet you place is −EV — and you won't know.

Pros and cons

Top-Down — Pros

  • Low effort per bet. Tools surface the spots.
  • Hard to be flat-out wrong — you're using the best estimate available.
  • Scales: you can place 50+ bets per day across many books.
  • Works without expertise in any specific sport.

Top-Down — Cons

  • You're hitting the same prices everyone using the same tools is hitting.
  • Limits drop fast. Books recognize the pattern.
  • Edge per bet is usually small (1-4%). Volume matters.
  • Useless when no sharp benchmark exists (niche markets, illiquid props).

Bottom-Up — Pros

  • Edges can be huge (5-15%+) on softer markets where market is thin.
  • Books struggle to flag you — your action looks like a sharp opinion, not a price-arb pattern.
  • Scales with the depth of your model, not with the number of books.
  • Works in places where top-down has no signal (NCAA, esports, in-game).

Bottom-Up — Cons

  • Model risk. You can be confidently wrong for months.
  • Requires sport-specific data and discipline to backtest.
  • Slow. Prop-by-prop work is hours of effort for a daily slate.
  • Easy to fool yourself. Variance can mask both edges and leaks.

Top-down works where the market is sharp. Bottom-up works where it isn't.

The same bettor can use both, on the same night. Top-down the NFL games, bottom-up the NCAA props your model covers. The wrong move is to use one approach in a market suited to the other — top-down on a market with no sharp benchmark, or bottom-up on a market where Pinnacle already nailed it.

The hybrid approach

Most full-time pros are some blend. They run a top-down operation across ~20 books for the easy edges, and they have one or two markets where they've built a real edge from scratch. The hybrid lets them:

  • Pay the bills with top-down. Steady volume, smaller per-bet edge, lots of books to spread the action.
  • Find the big scores with bottom-up. A pitcher-K model that's 4-5% sharper than market prices on its target slate produces edges other approaches can't reach.
  • Diversify the flagging risk. If books cap your top-down action, your bottom-up bets keep producing on books that haven't yet noticed.
Practical sequence: start top-down. It's the lower-risk on-ramp because the math is mechanical and you're not betting your own opinion against Pinnacle. Once you're consistently profitable and you've identified a market where you have edge or interest, layer bottom-up on top.

How to tell which one you're doing

Most casual bettors think they're bottom-up but they're really doing neither. Three quick checks:

  • Top-down test: Could you describe the bet you just placed in one sentence as "Pinnacle says 55%, this book is implying 48%, so I bet"? If yes — you're top-down.
  • Bottom-up test: Could you write down the projection your model produced and the inputs that led to it? If yes — you're bottom-up.
  • Neither test: Did you bet because "the matchup looks good" or "the spread feels off" or "I have a feeling about the under"? That's not a strategy. That's gambling on intuition. Variance will tell you you're great for two weeks, then take it all back.

Knowing which one you're doing — explicitly — is the difference between profitable betting and recreational betting wearing a profitability costume.

Caveats

  • Top-down isn't free. Books drop limits aggressively on top-down patterns. Your $200 bet today might cap at $11 next week. Plan for that.
  • Bottom-up isn't a license to bet your gut. A "model" written down in a notebook with no backtest is gut-feel with extra steps. The discipline is in the data, not the format.
  • Edge size lies. A 7% calculated edge means nothing if your model is biased. Check it against actual outcomes over hundreds of bets.
  • The market gets sharper over time. Edges that exist now (especially top-down at smaller books) won't last forever. Bottom-up edges in niche markets persist longer because fewer people are looking.
