Why 3 and 7 are the "key numbers"

NFL scores move in 3s and 7s, so final margins pile up on those numbers. Across every regular-season and playoff game from 2015 through 2025 (nflverse data, 3,028 games):

  • Exactly 3 — 14.8% of all games. One number, one game in seven.
  • Exactly 7 — 8.7%. Exactly 6 — 6.9%. Exactly 10 — 4.8%. Exactly 14 — 5.2%.
  • Exactly 5 or 9 — 4.4% and 1.7%. The numbers between the keys are dead zones.

That is why a half-point around 3 is worth far more than a half-point around 5, and why books charge more for it. The Landing Table shows the same idea priced for one specific game, straight from the market.

How we get a fair price for every number (the honest part)

Every book posts a ladder of alternate spreads — −2.5, −3, −3.5 … each with its own price. For each rung:

  • Take only books quoting both sides at that number, and de-vig each pair (power method) — that's the book's own fair probability the side covers. The Vig →
  • Take the median across books (or the sharp-book average, your toggle). A single stale quote can't drag it.
  • Force the curve to be monotone: a bigger cushion can never be less likely to cover than a smaller one. Tiny cross-book noise gets smoothed; the fit rarely moves a rung by more than a point.
  • P(margin lands on exactly 3) is then just P(covers −2.5) − P(covers −3.5). No model, no opinion — the difference between two market prices.

Half-point rungs only go into the curve (no push to muddy them). Whole-number prices — like −3 at −120 — are then checked against it; on this slate they reconcile within a few tenths of a percent.

Why the old teaser rules aren't enough anymore

The classic "Wong teaser" — tease a favourite of −7.5/−8.5 down through 7 and 3, or an underdog of +1.5/+2.5 up through 3 and 7 — was built from early-2000s data at −110 two-team pricing. Two things changed: most books now price two-team teasers at −120 to −135, and they shade the numbers around 3 and 7. A −120 two-team teaser needs each leg to win about 73.9% of the time before pushes; at −110, 72.4%.

So the only honest test is the one this tool runs: the market's fair chance at the teased number, leg by leg, against the break-even your book's payout demands. Some Wong legs clear it this week. Some don't. The rule of thumb can't tell you which — the ladder can. Parlays — how multi-leg pricing compounds →

What this tool can't tell you
  • It's a snapshot. Ladders are pulled several times a day before kickoff; the freshness stamp at the top is the truth. Prices move.
  • Far rungs are thin. A −20.5 alt may be quoted by two or three books. The ladder table flags how many books stand behind each rung.
  • It's the market's opinion, de-vigged — not a prediction. Beating it requires a reason the market is wrong, which is a different tool's job.
  • Teaser rules vary by book (push = reduce vs. push = void). Read yours.
