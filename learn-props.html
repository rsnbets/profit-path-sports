Prop Bets

A prop bet isn't on who wins — it's on a number a player or team produces. It's the market most casual bettors live in, and the one books price the loosest. How to read a prop line, why the numbers are tricky, and where the edges hide.

What a prop is

A prop — short for proposition bet — isn't a bet on the final score or who wins. It's a bet on a specific number a player or team produces, or on whether some event happens at all.

Three common kinds:

  • Player props — Patrick Mahomes over 2.5 passing TDs, LeBron over 27.5 points, a pitcher over 6.5 strikeouts.
  • Team props — the Lakers' team total over 112.5, a team to score first.
  • Game props — will the game go to overtime, will there be a defensive or special-teams TD.

Casual bettors love props because they're tied to watching one player do one thing. That's also exactly why books are happy to offer thousands of them — most are bet on vibes, not numbers. Which is the opportunity, once you can read them properly.

Reading a prop line — the notation trap

Here's the thing that trips up new bettors: the same bet gets written several different ways, and books aren't consistent about which they show.

"Over 16.5 points" and "17+ points" are the identical bet. Both win on 17 or more, both lose on 16 or fewer. One book lists a half-point line, another lists a "plus" threshold — same wager, different label.

The same bet, three ways of writing it Over 16.5  =  17+  =  needs 17 or more
Under 16.5  =  16 or fewer  =  needs 16 or fewer

The part that actually costs people money is the difference between a half-point line and a whole-number line. A half-point line (16.5) can never tie — every result is either over or under it. A whole-number line (17) can tie: if the player lands on exactly 17, the bet pushes and your stake is refunded.

Watch the half-point. "Over 17" and "Over 16.5" look almost identical but they are not the same bet — "Over 17" needs 18, and a result of exactly 17 gives you your money back instead of a win. Always confirm whether the number you're betting is a whole number or a half-point before you compare prices across books.

Props don't land on every number

A side or total is the sum of a whole game's scoring, so it behaves almost like a smooth, continuous number. Props often don't. A single player's stat line is discrete and chunky — it jumps in steps, and some numbers are far more common than others.

Receiving yards land all over the place, so a yards prop is nearly smooth. But receptions, made threes, touchdowns, strikeouts come in small whole numbers — and the gap between "Over 0.5 TDs" and "Over 1.5 TDs" is an enormous jump in probability, not a small one.

This is why alternate prop lines have to be priced individually rather than slid up and down by a fixed amount — and why a "wide-looking" gap on a discrete stat can be far narrower in real probability than it looks. The Middles lesson breaks down the smooth-vs-clustered distinction in full; it applies directly to props.

Props are soft — and that cuts both ways

A sportsbook puts its sharpest pricing on the main side and total of big games — that's where the most money moves and where sharp bettors attack. But it also offers thousands of player props every night, and it can't price them all with the same care.

That makes props the softest market on the board. Prop lines move slower, lag injury and role news longer, and are more often simply wrong. For a bettor who isn't a full-time pro, props are where a real edge is most likely to be found.

But "soft" doesn't mean "free." Two costs come with the territory:

  • Higher vig. Props routinely carry 6–8% vig or more — versus 4–5% on a mainline. Your edge has to clear a taller hurdle just to break even.
  • Over-only listings. Many props only show the Over price on screen. To devig one you need both sides — pull the Under from the alt ladder or from a sharper book.

And one more, covered in the Key Point below: props are the fastest way to get your account limited.

What sharps do about props

  • They devig a sharp book, not the one they're betting. A prop line at a soft book (BetMGM, Bet365) isn't the fair price — they devig the same prop at a sharp book (Pinnacle, Circa) to get the true number, then check whether the soft book's price beats it.
  • They line-shop hard. Prop lines vary between books far more than mainlines do — the same player can be Over 23.5 at one book and Over 25.5 at another. Half a point on a prop is a real edge.
  • They demand a bigger edge. Because the vig is higher, a prop needs more margin than a mainline before it clears +EV.
  • They expect limits and spread their action. Books profile prop bettors aggressively — a winning prop player gets cut faster than anyone.

Props are where the soft lines live — which means they're also where the books are watching hardest.

★ Key Point — Props Are Soft, Not Free
Props are the loosest-priced market a sportsbook offers, so for most bettors they're the most likely place to find a real edge. But two things eat into it: the vig runs 6–8%+ instead of 4–5%, so your edge has to be bigger to matter — and consistent prop winners get their accounts limited faster than anywhere else, because books watch this market closely. So treat props as the opportunity they are, but earn it: devig a sharp book for the true number, demand an edge that clears the higher juice, and don't expect a winning prop account to stay un-limited for long.
