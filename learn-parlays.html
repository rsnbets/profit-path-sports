Parlays & SGPs

Sportsbooks make most of their per-bet margin on parlays. The reason isn't conspiracy — it's compounded vig across legs, and same-game parlays add a hidden markup on top of that. The math behind why parlays are usually −EV, why SGPs are even worse than they look, and the rare cases when a parlay actually makes sense.

What is a parlay?

A parlay is a single bet that combines multiple legs into one ticket. To win, every leg has to hit. One leg loses, the whole ticket dies.

Two flavors:

  • Cross-game parlay — legs from different games or events. Outcomes are independent: the Lakers winning has nothing to do with whether the Yankees go over 8.5.
  • Same-game parlay (SGP) — legs from the same game. Outcomes are correlated: if Saquon Barkley goes for 100+ rushing yards, the Eagles are more likely to win.

Recreational bettors love parlays because the payout looks huge for a small stake. Sharp bettors avoid them because the math is brutal — and SGPs are even worse than they look on the screen.

How parlay odds compound — and the vig with them

Cross-game parlay odds are simple multiplication. Convert each leg's American odds to decimal, multiply them all together, that's your combined decimal price.

Four-leg parlay of −110 lines Leg 1: −110 → 1.909
Leg 2: −110 → 1.909
Leg 3: −110 → 1.909
Leg 4: −110 → 1.909
─────────────────────
Combined decimal: 1.909⁴ = 13.30
American: ~+1230

Looks generous. But each of those −110 lines bakes in the book's vig — the line implies 52.38% probability versus true 50% on a coin flip. That tiny per-leg edge for the book compounds multiplicatively.

Expected loss per $1 wagered (assuming true 50% legs) Single bet at −110: −4.55% expected loss
2-leg parlay (−110 × 2): −8.9% expected loss
4-leg parlay (−110 × 4): −16.9% expected loss
8-leg parlay (−110 × 8): −30.8% expected loss

Same picks, more vig. Every leg you stack roughly compounds the book's expected take by another 4.5%. That's why a 4-leg parlay is mathematically worse than placing four singles — even with identical lines and identical opinions on the games.

To make a parlay actually +EV, you need an edge on every single leg that beats the compounded vig. One unsharp leg drags the whole ticket below break-even.

The same-game parlay markup

SGPs are the worst version of a bad idea — and not because of the legs themselves. It's because of how books price them.

When you build a same-game parlay, the book doesn't just multiply the leg odds together. They can't — those odds were priced as standalone bets, and the legs of an SGP are correlated. So the book runs its own SGP pricing engine that adjusts for correlation.

And while they're at it, they add extra vig. A lot of it.

Illustrative SGP markup — Eagles favored, Barkley anytime TD Eagles ML: −200 → decimal 1.500
Barkley anytime TD: +100 → decimal 2.000
──────────────────────────
Naive multiplication: 1.500 × 2.000 = 3.00 (American +200)
Typical SGP price: ~+150 to +175 (decimal 2.50-2.75)
──────────────────────────
Hidden SGP markup: ~10-20% worse than naive multiply

The book's official explanation is "correlation" — Eagles winning makes Barkley scoring more likely, so the legs aren't independent and the math has to account for that. Partially true. But sharp analysis of SGP pricing across major books consistently shows the effective vig on SGPs is roughly 10-15% versus 4-5% on singles. Most of the "correlation discount" is just book margin.

The bottom line on SGPs: they look enticing because the screen shows you a glossy combined payout, and you assume that payout reflects "what the legs are worth." It doesn't. SGP odds are systematically worse than a naive cross-game multiply, by a margin that swallows whatever edge you thought you had on the individual picks.

What sharps do about parlays

  • They place straights, not parlays. If they like 4 picks, they bet 4 singles. Same picks, no compounded vig.
  • They avoid SGPs as a default. The combination of compounded vig plus SGP markup means most SGPs are heavily −EV even with correlation in your favor.
  • They hunt for SGP mispricings. These exist — rare cases where the book underestimates correlation (e.g., a team likely to be throwing in the 4th quarter combined with their QB's passing yards over). Sharps who specialize in SGPs build models for these specific spots.
  • They use the parlay calc to disprove parlays they're tempted by, not to confirm them. If they can't articulate exactly which leg has +EV and how that EV survives the compounded vig, they pass.

If you can't tell me how each leg is +EV individually, your parlay isn't a sharp bet. It's a gut play with a glossy payout.

The exception: bonus-bet parlays. Since you're not risking your own cash, the math flips — long-odds parlays maximize bonus-bet conversion. That's a different playbook, covered in Path 02.
