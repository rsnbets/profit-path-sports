Parlays & SGPs

Sportsbooks earn more per dollar on parlays than on any other bet they offer. It's not a conspiracy — it's vig compounding across every leg you stack. Why parlays are usually −EV, why same-game parlays are worse, and the rare time they're not.

What a parlay is

A parlay is a single bet that combines multiple legs onto one ticket. To win, every leg has to hit. One leg loses and the whole ticket dies.

Two flavors:

  • Cross-game parlay — legs from different games. The outcomes are independent: the Lakers winning has nothing to do with whether the Yankees go over 8.5.
  • Same-game parlay (SGP) — legs from one game. The outcomes are correlated: if Saquon Barkley runs for 100+ yards, the Eagles are more likely to have won.

Recreational bettors love parlays because the payout looks huge for a small stake. Sharp bettors avoid them because the math is brutal — and SGPs are worse than they look on the screen.

How parlay odds compound — and the vig with them

Cross-game parlay odds are simple multiplication. Convert each leg's American odds to decimal, multiply them together, and that's your combined price.

A four-leg parlay of −110 lines Leg 1: −110 → 1.909
Leg 2: −110 → 1.909
Leg 3: −110 → 1.909
Leg 4: −110 → 1.909
─────────────────────────
Combined decimal: 1.909⁴  =  13.30
American: ~+1230

Looks generous. But each −110 line bakes in the book's vig — the price implies a 52.38% chance versus a true 50% on a coin flip. That tiny per-leg edge for the book compounds multiplicatively.

Expected loss per $1 wagered (assuming true 50% legs) Single bet at −110        −4.55%
2-leg parlay (−110 × 2)   −8.9%
4-leg parlay (−110 × 4)   −17.0%
8-leg parlay (−110 × 8)   −31.1%

Same picks, more vig. Every leg you stack compounds the book's expected take by roughly another 4.5%. That's why a 4-leg parlay is mathematically worse than placing four singles — identical lines, identical opinions, worse result.

To make a parlay actually +EV you need an edge on every single leg big enough to beat the compounded vig. One unsharp leg drags the whole ticket below break-even.

The same-game parlay markup

SGPs are the worst version of a bad idea — and not because of the legs themselves. It's because of how books price them.

When you build a same-game parlay, the book doesn't just multiply the leg odds together. It can't — those odds were priced as standalone bets, and the legs of an SGP are correlated. So the book runs its own SGP pricing engine that adjusts for correlation.

And while it's at it, it adds extra vig. A lot of it.

Illustrative SGP markup — Eagles favored, Barkley anytime TD Eagles ML:          −200 → decimal 1.500
Barkley anytime TD:  +100 → decimal 2.000
──────────────────────────────
Naive multiplication:  1.500 × 2.000 = 3.00  (American +200)
Typical SGP price:     ~+150 to +175  (decimal 2.50–2.75)
──────────────────────────────
Hidden SGP markup:    ~10–20% worse than a naive multiply

The book's official explanation is "correlation" — an Eagles win makes a Barkley score more likely, so the legs aren't independent and the math has to adjust. Partly true. But sharp analysis of SGP pricing across the major books consistently shows the effective vig on SGPs runs roughly 10–15% versus 4–5% on singles. Most of the "correlation discount" is just book margin.

The bottom line on SGPs: they look enticing because the screen shows you a glossy combined payout, and you assume that number reflects what the legs are worth. It doesn't. SGP odds are systematically worse than a naive cross-game multiply — by a margin that swallows whatever edge you thought you had on the individual picks.

What sharps do about parlays

  • They place straights, not parlays. If they like four picks, they bet four singles — same picks, no compounded vig.
  • They avoid SGPs by default. Compounded vig plus SGP markup means most same-game parlays are heavily −EV even with correlation in your favor.
  • They hunt for SGP mispricings. These exist — rare spots where the book underestimates correlation (a team likely to be throwing late in a blowout paired with their QB's passing-yards over). Sharps who specialize in SGPs build models for exactly those spots.
  • They use a parlay calculator to disprove parlays they're tempted by, not to confirm them. If they can't name which leg has +EV and show that edge survives the compounded vig, they pass.

If you can't tell me how each leg is +EV on its own, your parlay isn't a sharp bet — it's a gut play with a glossy payout.

One real exception: bonus-bet parlays. When you're not risking your own cash, the math flips — long-odds parlays actually maximize what a bonus bet converts to. That's a different playbook, covered in Path 02 · Convert.

★ Key Point — Stack Legs, Stack Vig
A parlay doesn't create an edge — it multiplies the book's edge. Each leg you add compounds the vig by roughly another 4.5%, so a clean 4-leg ticket of −110 picks is a −17% bet where four separate singles would each be −4.55%. Same opinions, far worse price. The honest test before any parlay: can you explain, leg by leg, why each one beats its own no-vig price? If not — and for nearly every parlay, you can't — bet the legs you like as straights and leave the glossy combined payout alone. (The lone exception is a bonus bet, where it isn't your money on the line.)
