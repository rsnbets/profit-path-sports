Concept · Futures Strategy

Futures Betting

A futures ticket isn't a bet you place and forget — it's a position you manage as the season unfolds. The single rule that turns futures from gambling into portfolio management: always split your tickets across books, and at least one of those books must allow cashouts.

What futures are

A futures bet is a wager on an outcome that resolves at the end of a season, tournament, or postseason — not at the end of a single game. Common examples:

  • Award winners — Cy Young, MVP, Coach of the Year
  • Division / conference winners
  • Championships — Stanley Cup, World Series, Super Bowl
  • Win totals — team season wins above/below a posted line
  • Player season stats — most home runs, most rushing yards

The horizon is what makes them different. A spread bet settles in 3 hours; a Cy Young ticket settles in 5-6 months. The price moves continuously over that period as evidence accumulates — strong starts, injuries, trade-deadline moves, postseason positioning. Your position changes value every day, even on days the player isn't pitching.

The cashout mechanic — and its haircut

Most US-facing books (DraftKings, FanDuel, BetMGM, Caesars, Hard Rock, ESPN BET, etc.) offer cashout on futures tickets. At any point before settlement, the book will quote you a price to buy back your ticket. Take it, and your bet is settled immediately for that cash amount — locked in, no further variance.

The math: a fair cashout = current implied probability × total payout if the bet wins. If your $10 ticket pays $260 total and the current implied probability of winning is 25%, fair cashout = $65. The book never offers fair. They quote you something below — a haircut, typically 5-20% — that's their compensation for warehousing your position and absorbing the risk.

Fair vs offered cashout — typical structure
Total payout$260
Current implied prob25%
Fair cashout$260 × 0.25 = $65.00
Book's offered cashout$57.24~12% haircut
Cashout never gives you fair value. It gives you convenience at a cost. The question on every cashout is whether the convenience is worth the haircut.

The haircut size varies by book and by how confident the book is in its model. On well-priced markets (MLB award futures, NFL win totals), haircuts run 5-10%. On thin markets (NHL conference futures, niche awards), haircuts can be 15-25%.

The rule: always split your tickets across books

If you only place a single futures ticket at a single book, you've handed yourself zero optionality. The price you got is the price you're stuck with. The cashout offer (if your book even allows it) is the only exit you have. Every flexibility lever — price shopping, hedging, partial closeouts — disappears.

The rule is simple: any futures position worth caring about should be split across at least two books. And one of those books must allow cashouts.

A single futures ticket is a one-way bet. Two tickets at different books is a position you can manage. The split costs you nothing on the way in and saves you everything on the way out.

Why split?

  • Price shopping. Futures prices vary meaningfully across books — sometimes 30-50% on long-shot tickets. A $10 ticket might pay $260 at one book and $205 at another. Splitting lets you grab the best of each.
  • Limit / promo capture. Books offer odds boosts on futures regularly. Having accounts at 3-5 books means you can grab the boost whenever it appears, instead of being locked to one book's mood.
  • Diversification. If one book limits or restricts your account mid-season, your futures position survives. You're not stuck.
  • Cashout flexibility — the key one, next section.

Why one book MUST allow cashouts

This is the part most bettors miss. The sharpest futures prices live at sharp / exchange books — Pinnacle, ProphetX, Novig, Circa. Those venues don't offer cashout. Once your ticket is placed, you're holding it to settlement no matter what happens.

That's fine if the underlying never moves. It's a problem the moment circumstances change:

  • Your pitcher dominates the first half and the price implodes. You want to lock in some of the gain. You can't — Pinnacle doesn't offer that.
  • The injury rumor hits and the price collapses. You want out before the official report. You can't.
  • You hit a great cashout price right before a big start that could swing the line either way. You can't take it.

The fix: place a portion of your futures position at a soft book that offers cashouts — DraftKings, FanDuel, BetMGM, Caesars, Hard Rock, ESPN BET, etc. The sharp book gives you the best price; the soft book gives you the exit lever. Together they form a position you can actually manage.

Practical setup: when you're building a futures position you actually care about, aim for at least two tickets — one at a sharp book for the best price, one at a soft book for the cashout option. Even if the soft book's odds are 10-15% worse, the optionality alone is worth it on long-horizon positions.

One trap: don't place the same futures bet twice at the same book

Some books revoke the cashout option entirely if you place the same futures ticket twice on a single account. They flag the duplicate as suspicious — even if it's legitimately you adding to a position — and the result is your ticket gets locked to settlement with no exit lever.

  • DraftKings: confirmed to do this. Doubling up the same ticket at DK kills your cashout option on that bet. Don't do it if you want to keep the lever.
  • Hard Rock Bet: does NOT do this. You can stack the same ticket at HR without losing cashout.
  • Other books (FanDuel, BetMGM, Caesars, ESPN BET, bet365, Fanatics): untested. Assume the worst until you've confirmed otherwise on a small ticket.

The fix: if you want to add to a futures position, place the additional ticket at a different book — not at the same one where your original lives. Each ticket keeps its own cashout option, and you pick up the price-shopping benefit on top.

Sharp books with no cashout: Pinnacle, Circa, ProphetX, Novig, Bookmaker. Soft books with cashout: DraftKings, FanDuel, BetMGM, Caesars, Hard Rock, ESPN BET, bet365, Fanatics. Stagger accordingly.

Cashout vs. hedge — two ways to lock in

If a futures price has moved in your favor and you want to lock in some profit, you have two tools:

  1. Cashout. Click one button at the book that holds your ticket. Immediate, simple, accepts the haircut.
  2. Hedge. Bet the OPPOSITE outcome at current market prices, sized to lock in equal profit (or a chosen profit floor) regardless of which side wins. Use our hedge calculator to size it.

When is hedging better than cashing?

  • When the book's cashout markup is large (15%+) and you can find a cleaner hedge price elsewhere
  • When the outcome has multiple paths (e.g., Cy Young has many contenders) and you can hedge across just the most likely competitors instead of "everyone else"
  • When the cashout isn't available at all (sharp-book ticket, no cashout option)

When is cashing simpler/better?

  • When the haircut is small (under 10%) and you don't want to manage another bet
  • When the field is wide and hedging would require many separate bets
  • When you want the position fully closed (cashout = exit; hedge = both sides still open)

Walkthrough — a 3-ticket Cy Young futures portfolio

Here's a futures portfolio in motion. Pitcher's getting strong early-season buzz, his Cy Young price has tightened, and the bettor is staring at three tickets across different books, each with a cashout offer. All stakes shown in units (u) — 1u = whatever stake size matches your bankroll, typically 1-2% of total roll.

The position
Ticket A1u → 25u · cashout ~5ucashout/return ≈ 20%
Ticket B1u → 22u · cashout ~4.5ucashout/return ≈ 20%
Ticket C0.5u → 20u · cashout ~4ucashout/return ≈ 20%
Total stake2.5u
Max total return67u (if he wins)
Total cashout now~13.5u · ~5× original stake locked in

All three tickets imply the book sees his current Cy Young probability at ~20% (after the haircut). That's a significant move from where they were bought — Ticket C was originally a much longer shot (the implied prob has roughly 8× from its entry).

The "all or nothing" trap

Bettor reflex: cash out everything for the guaranteed 13.5u, or hold everything for the potential 67u. Both are bad. The first throws away most of the upside; the second exposes the full stake to a single bad start or injury.

The barbell approach is better. Cash out the ticket in the middle. Keep the extremes.

Barbell: cash the middle ticket
CashTicket B (1u → 22u, cashout 4.5u)
HoldTicket A (biggest max payout, 25u) — biggest upside if he wins
HoldTicket C (longest original odds, 20u on 0.5u stake) — best ROI lottery slip
Locked-in profit+3.5u on 4.5u cashout − 1u original stake on B
Remaining stake at risk1.5u (1u on A + 0.5u on C)
Max remaining payout45u (25u from A + 20u from C) if he wins
Cashing Ticket B converts 1u of stake into a 3.5u locked profit. You still have 1.5u working with 45u of upside. The "free roll" component is now real — even if both remaining tickets lose, you're up 3.5u net on the position.

Why the middle ticket?

Three reasons the middle ticket is usually the right one to cash:

  • Ticket A has the biggest max payout ($260). If Schlittler wins, this is the ticket you most regret cashing. Hold it for full upside.
  • Ticket C has the longest original odds ($5 stake → $205, ~9× implied-prob move so far). It's a free-roll lottery slip — the absolute dollars at risk are tiny relative to the original odds. Hold it for the lottery payout if his season keeps climbing.
  • Ticket B is the middle: meaningful cashout, doesn't sacrifice the most upside, doesn't waste the longest-shot ticket. It's the one whose risk/reward is the most "average" — and the easiest one to monetize without regret.

This is the same logic that gives barbell portfolios their name: keep the high-conviction extreme (Ticket A, the biggest payout) and the lottery extreme (Ticket C, the longest shot), monetize the middle (Ticket B).

The harder case — multi-ticket playoff futures

Futures get genuinely complex when you have multiple tickets across multiple correlated outcomes. Here's a stylized example based on a real NHL playoff scenario:

The position, mid-Round 2
Team M · East1u → 15u (cashout ~3u) · 1u → 14u (cashout ~2.5u)
Team B · East0.2u → 1.6u (no cashout)
Team C · Cup0.5u → 10u
Team B · Cup0.5u → 10.5u
SituationM leads B 2-1 in R2 · Team H already through, dominant in playoffs, sitting around −250 to win East · Team H wasn't hedged

The structural problem

The position is exposed to a path the bettor never priced: Team H winning the East. H is the heaviest favorite (−250, ~71% implied), the bettor has no H exposure, and H taking the East would void every East ticket they hold. The Team-C and Team-B Cup tickets also become long shots if M/B fall out of the East entirely.

Three decisions present themselves:

  1. Cash the M tickets to lock in something. Total M cashout: ~5.5u. That's roughly +3.5u of locked profit on 2u of stake. Removes the "M loses to B anyway, then can't beat H" risk.
  2. Plug the H gap. Bet ~2.5u on Team H −250 to win the East. Pays ~1u. That partially offsets the regret of the East tickets voiding if H wins.
  3. Hold and pray. Live with the variance. M/B need to advance through R2 AND beat H AND then win the Cup.

How to think about it

Lay out the scenarios that could happen and what each one nets:

Scenario tree — current position, no further action
ScenarioEast champNet result
M beats B, M beats H, M wins CupM15u + 14u = 29u max (no M Cup ticket)
M beats B, M beats H, C wins CupM15u + 14u + 10u = 39u
H wins East (most likely)HAll East tickets void → −2.2u (East stakes lost)
B beats M, B beats H, B wins CupB1.6u + 10.5u = 12.1u − 2u M stake = +10u net

The biggest risk is the most likely outcome: H winning the East. The M tickets are net −2u in that scenario. The C Cup ticket survives because C isn't dependent on East path, but it's still a long shot.

What the cashout buys you

Cashing both M tickets locks in +3.5u profit (cashouts total ~5.5u minus 2u in original stakes). That's a meaningful number that doesn't depend on M beating B or H. The cost: forgoing the upside if M goes all the way (max 29-39u in those branches).

If you believe H is roughly the −250 favorite the line suggests (~71% implied), then M's win-the-East probability is maybe 8-12%. The M tickets' expected value going forward is roughly 29u × 0.10 = 2.9u — less than the 3.5u locked-in profit from cashing. The cashout is mathematically favorable here because the implied path to M winning is too narrow to justify holding.

The lesson the NHL example surfaces: futures portfolios drift into structural exposure you didn't intend. The CAR gap was a leftover from a position built when the East field was more open. As the field narrows in the playoffs, gaps become outright risks. Cashout/hedge decisions aren't just "lock in profit?" — they're also "fix the gap that's accumulated as the season has unfolded."

Portfolio construction for futures

Practical rules for placing futures bets you'll need to manage later:

  • Always at least two books per outcome. One sharp for the best price, one soft for the cashout option.
  • Stagger your entries. Don't dump your full stake at one price point. As evidence accumulates, your fair-value estimate sharpens — buying multiple times over the season lets you average into a better effective price.
  • Match your stakes to your conviction. If you believe the implied prob is way off, scale up. If you're chasing a hunch, scale down. Most bettors over-stake on hunches and under-stake on edges.
  • Map the scenario tree before you have to. Before postseason starts, know which outcomes void your tickets and which compound them. The CAR gap above was foreseeable — it just wasn't foreseen.
  • Set cashout triggers, not cashout impulses. Decide in advance: "if my cashout reaches $X, I'll close." Otherwise you'll either cash too early (every small uptick feels urgent) or too late (you'll forget to revisit).
  • Don't ignore correlated futures. A Cy Young ticket and an under-the-team-win-total ticket can be partially correlated. A Cup winner and Conference winner are highly correlated. Track your portfolio's net exposure, not each ticket in isolation.

Caveats

  • Cashout markups aren't published. The book doesn't tell you their haircut directly. You have to estimate it by comparing cashout to your own implied probability — which is exactly what most bettors don't bother to do.
  • Partial cashout is sometimes offered. Some books let you cash out a portion of your ticket and let the rest ride. Useful, but the partial-cashout haircut is usually steeper than the full-cashout haircut.
  • "No cashout available right now" doesn't mean the position can't be exited. Books pause cashout during volatile moments (post-news, mid-game). It usually reopens once the line stabilizes.
  • Hedging across books carries tax / accounting friction. Wins at multiple books are reported separately for tax purposes; losses to offset depend on jurisdiction. Doesn't change the EV math but does affect post-tax outcomes.
  • Long-shot futures are emotional positions. If you've been holding a longshot ticket for 5 months and it's finally paying real cashout dollars, the cashout urge can be hard to resist even when holding is the right move. Pre-committed exit rules help here.

How this fits with the rest of the framework

  • The hedge calculator sizes the "bet the other side" play when you'd rather hedge than cash out.
  • CLV tracking applies to futures the same way it does to straight bets — beating the closing line is the leading indicator of long-run profit on futures portfolios too.
  • Kelly sizing for futures is harder because the resolution is far in the future and variance compounds with time. Use lower Kelly fractions (eighth or sixteenth) for long-shot futures than you would for straight bets.
  • Cross-market EV ideas apply to futures too — when a soft book has a futures price meaningfully better than the sharp consensus, that's the same +EV opportunity, just with a longer settle horizon.
