What is a middle?

A "middle" is when you bet both sides at different numbers — and there's a gap where both win.

Two books disagree on the number for the same market. You take the over at one book and the under at another, both at numbers wide enough apart that there's a real chance the result lands between them. If it does, you cash both tickets.

The downside: you lose the difference in juice when the result lands outside the middle. The upside: when it hits, you win both sides — often a 10x+ return on the bet you exposed.

Example: bet Over 47.5 at one book and Under 50.5 at another. If the total comes in at 48, 49, or 50, both win — the middle landed. If it's 47 or lower, only the under wins. If it's 51 or higher, only the over wins.

How to use

Sizing the middle right

  1. Enter Side A. Your stake and the American odds at the first book (e.g., Over 47.5 at -110).
  2. Enter Side B. The other side of the gap at the second book (e.g., Under 50.5 at -105). Leave "Auto-size Side B" checked and we'll compute the stake that makes the worst-case loss equal on both sides — the textbook approach.
  3. Read the scenarios. Three columns show your net P&L in each outcome: A wins outside the middle, the middle hits and both cash, B wins outside the middle. The "worst-case loss" stat is your maximum downside; the "best case" is the payoff if the middle lands.
  4. Optional — add a middle-hit probability. If you have an estimate of how often the result lands in the gap (typically based on sharp-line implied probability), the EV row shows the expected dollar and percent return per bet. The "break-even middle prob" tells you how often the middle has to hit before the bet is +EV.
Realistic middle probabilities: NFL totals rarely middle (1-3% on standard 3-point gaps); NBA half-point middles cluster around 5-8%; MLB run totals with multi-run gaps are higher. Don't guess — pull the probability from a sharp book's price on the exact result range.
