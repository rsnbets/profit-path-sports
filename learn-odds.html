Reading the Odds

Every bet at a sportsbook is shown as a number — like −150 or +130. You choose what to bet — that number sets how much you'd win for it. Reading it is the very first skill — and we'll start from zero.

Start with the simplest bet

Forget anything fancy for a minute. The most basic bet in sports is this: pick which team wins the game. Not the final score, not how much they win by — just the winner.

Open a betting app and you'll see the two teams, each with a number next to it:

Two teams, two numbers Lakers     −150
Celtics    +130

That number is the team's price. It does not tell you how much to bet — how much you put down is always your choice. What it tells you is the deal: for whatever you decide to risk, how much you'd get back if you're right.

One team's number has a minus sign, the other has a plus sign. That sign isn't decoration — it changes how you read the number. The next two sections take them one at a time.

One quick label: on a betting app, this "pick the winner" bet is usually called the moneyline. So when you see that word, it just means the plain who-wins bet you're learning right here.

The minus sign: the favorite

A minus number marks the favorite — the team more likely to win the game.

Here's how to read it. The minus number is how many dollars you'd have to bet to win $100:

How to read a minus number −150  →  bet $150  to win $100
−200  →  bet $200  to win $100
−400  →  bet $400  to win $100

Why does the favorite cost so much? Because it's the more likely winner — so the sportsbook makes you risk more to win the same amount. The bigger the minus number, the bigger the favorite.

One thing to be clear about up front: $100 is just an easy reference number — it's used here because it makes the math obvious. You don't have to bet $100, or $150, or any particular amount. Bet whatever you're comfortable with and the payout scales right along with it. Put $15 on the Lakers at −150 and you'd win $10 instead of $100 — same shape, smaller numbers.

And whenever a bet wins, you get your own money back on top of your winnings. So a winning $150 bet at −150 puts $250 in your account — your $150 back, plus the $100 you won.

The plus sign: the underdog

A plus number marks the underdog — the team less likely to win.

You read it the other way around. The plus number is how much you'd win if you bet $100:

How to read a plus number +130  →  bet $100  to win $130
+200  →  bet $100  to win $200
+400  →  bet $100  to win $400

Why does the underdog pay so well? Because it's less likely to win — so the sportsbook pays you more to take that risk. The bigger the plus number, the bigger the underdog, and the bigger the reward if it comes through.

Same as the favorite: bet any amount you like and the payout scales to match. And you still get your own money back when you win — a winning $100 bet at +130 puts $230 in your account, your $100 back plus $130 won.

You'll see odds written two other ways

The plus/minus style above has a name — American odds — and it's what US sportsbooks use, so it's what you'll see most. But the exact same price can be written two other ways. You don't have to use them; you just want to recognize them.

  • Decimal odds — a single number, like 2.00. It's the total you'd get back for every $1 you bet, your own dollar included. A $1 bet at 2.00 returns $2.00. Common outside the US, and the easiest style for doing math.
  • Fractional odds — written as a fraction, like 5/1 (say it "five to one"). It's winnings over your bet: risk $1, win $5. Mostly used in the UK and in horse racing.
The same price, three ways of writing it −150  =  1.67 decimal  =  2/3 fractional
+100  =  2.00 decimal  =  1/1 fractional
+200  =  3.00 decimal  =  2/1 fractional

Why this is the lesson everything builds on

Reading the price is step one. But there's a second thing hiding inside that number, and it's where the rest of this path begins.

A price isn't only "bet this, win that." It's also the sportsbook's guess at how likely that team is to win. A big favorite at −400 is the book saying "this team almost always wins." A long underdog at +400 is the book saying "this one rarely does." The number is a prediction in disguise.

Here's the catch, and it's the reason this whole path exists: the sportsbook doesn't hand you those predictions straight. It shades every number slightly in its own favor — a small, built-in cut that is the reason most bettors slowly lose. You don't need the math for that yet. Just know that the line you see is never a perfectly fair number — and the lessons ahead teach you how to spot the cut and bet around it.

★ Key Point — A Price Is Also a Prediction
Every number on a sportsbook does two jobs at once: it tells you the payout, and it quietly reveals how likely the book thinks that team is to win. The bigger a favorite's minus number, the more sure the book is. That's the foundation of everything that follows — because smart betting isn't really about picking teams, it's about spotting when the book's number is wrong.
