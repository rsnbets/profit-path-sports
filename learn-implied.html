Implied Probability

Every odds price is a percentage in disguise. Learn to read it.

What is it?

In Lesson 01 you learned what odds say about dollars — risk vs. payout. Now we flip the same number on its side and read what it says about probability.

Every American odds price secretly contains a percentage: the chance the book is pricing this side to win. That number is its implied probability. A line of −110 isn't just "$110 to win $100." It's the book saying "we're pricing this as a 52.38% chance."

Once you can see the probability behind the price, you stop thinking about bets in dollars and start asking the only question that matters: do I think this side will win MORE often than the book is pricing?

The math behind it

Two formulas — one for each sign. They look fiddly at first, then become muscle memory.

Minus odds → implied probability prob = |odds| / (|odds| + 100)

−110 → 110 / 210 = 52.38%
−150 → 150 / 250 = 60.00%
−300 → 300 / 400 = 75.00% (big favorite)
Plus odds → implied probability prob = 100 / (odds + 100)

+110 → 100 / 210 = 47.62%
+200 → 100 / 300 = 33.33%
+400 → 100 / 500 = 20.00% (big underdog)

Bigger favorite, higher implied probability. Bigger underdog, lower. The number is just the book's stated guess at how often each side wins.

Why it matters more than you think

Remember the coin flip from Lesson 01? A real coin is exactly 50/50 — both sides win half the time, no exceptions.

So what would fair odds for a coin flip look like?

Fair Odds
+100 / +100
→
Implied Prob
50% / 50%

Both sides at +100, implying 50% each, summing to a clean 100%. Perfect. That's what "fair" looks like in math.

But that's not what the book actually posts on a coin flip. It posts −110 / −110. Run those through the formula and watch what happens:

A "coin flip" line at the sportsbook −110 → 52.38%
−110 → 52.38%
────────────────────
Total implied probability = 104.76%

The book's prices add up to more than 100%. That's mathematically impossible — no event can have more than a 100% total chance of happening. Two sides of a coin flip can't both be 52.38% likely to win.

So where did the extra 4.76% come from? It's not a math error. It's the next lesson.

What sharps do about it

They never look at −110 and think "$110 to win $100." They look at −110 and think "52.38%."

Then they ask one question: does this side win more than 52.38% of the time?

If yes → bet. If no → pass. Whether the price is in dollars, decimal, or American odds is just window dressing on the same question: am I getting a number that beats the true probability?

That's the entire game from here on out.
