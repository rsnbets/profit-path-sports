Implied Probability

Every price hides a percentage — the win rate the book is charging you to beat. Most bettors stare at the number and never see the percentage underneath. Learn to translate, and you stop betting dollars and start betting probabilities.

What it is

In Lesson 01 you learned what a price says about dollars — risk versus payout. Now flip the same number on its side and read what it says about probability.

Every price secretly contains a percentage: the chance the book is pricing that side to win. That's its implied probability. A line of −110 isn't just "$110 to win $100." It's the book saying "we're pricing this as a 52.38% chance."

Once you can see the probability behind the price, you stop thinking in dollars and start asking the only question that matters: do I think this side wins more often than the book is pricing it?

The math behind it

Two formulas — one for each sign. They feel fiddly the first few times, then become muscle memory.

Minus odds → implied probability prob = |odds| ÷ ( |odds| + 100 )

−110  →  110 ÷ 210  =  52.38%
−150  →  150 ÷ 250  =  60.00%
−300  →  300 ÷ 400  =  75.00%  (big favorite)
Plus odds → implied probability prob = 100 ÷ ( odds + 100 )

+110  →  100 ÷ 210  =  47.62%
+200  →  100 ÷ 300  =  33.33%
+400  →  100 ÷ 500  =  20.00%  (big underdog)

Bigger favorite, higher implied probability. Bigger underdog, lower. The percentage is just the book's stated guess at how often that side wins. (Decimal odds make it even simpler — implied probability is just 1 ÷ decimal odds.)

The 104.76% problem

Picture a coin flip. A real coin is exactly 50/50 — each side wins half the time, no exceptions. So what would fair odds for a coin flip look like?

Fair Odds
+100 / +100
→
Implied Prob
50% / 50%

Both sides at +100, implying 50% each, summing to a clean 100%. That's what "fair" looks like in math.

But that's not what a sportsbook posts on a coin-flip-style market. It posts −110 / −110. Run those through the formula and watch what happens:

A "coin flip" line at the sportsbook −110  →  52.38%
−110  →  52.38%
──────────────────────
Total implied probability  =  104.76%

The book's prices add up to more than 100%. That's impossible — no event can have more than a 100% total chance of happening. Both sides of a coin flip can't each be 52.38% likely to win.

So where did the extra 4.76% come from? It's not a math error. It's the sportsbook's fee — and it's the entire next lesson, The Vig.

What sharps do about it

They never look at −110 and think "$110 to win $100." They look at −110 and think "52.38%."

Then they ask one question: does this side win more often than 52.38% of the time? If yes, there's a bet. If no, pass. Whether the price is shown in dollars, decimal, or American odds is just window dressing on the same question — am I getting a number that beats the true probability?

That's the whole game from here on. But there's a trap in it, and the Key Point below is the one thing to take away from this lesson.

★ Key Point — Implied % Is Not the True %
The implied probability of a single price is always a little too high — because the book's fee is baked inside it. −110 implies 52.38%, but the true chance on a real coin flip is 50%. That 2.38% gap per side is the fee. So treat a single side's implied probability as a rough screen, never a fact. To get the real number you have to strip the fee off both sides of the market — that's called devigging, and it's covered in The Vig and built into the Devig Calculator.
