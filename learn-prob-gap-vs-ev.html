Concept · Edge Math

Probability Gap vs. EV

A 5% probability gap is not 5% expected value. They look like the same number, they get used interchangeably, and treating them as the same will quietly cost you money on every dog you skip and every favorite you overweight.

Meet the two numbers

Probability gap is how far the book's implied probability is from the true probability. It's measured in probability points. "The fair number is 55%, this book is pricing it at 50% — that's a 5-point gap."

One thing before anything else: "the fair number" never comes from your gut. It comes from exactly two places — a model you've built and tested, or the market itself (a sharp book's price with the vig stripped out — that's devigging). "I think the Chiefs win this one" is not a probability; it's the feeling that keeps losing bettors losing. Everything below assumes your number came from one of those two sources.

Expected value (EV) is how much you actually expect to make per dollar staked. It's measured in return on stake. EV bakes in the price you're getting paid — the same probability gap on a +400 dog is worth far more in EV than the same gap on a −300 favorite.

Both are valid. They measure different things. Conflating them is the most common math mistake in +EV betting.

Why this actually works (in plain English)

🚶 THE WALK-TO-LUNCH ROUTINE

You decide to walk to lunch instead of driving every workday. Each walk saves you $10 (gas + parking). About 10% of days it rains and you Uber for $20 instead.

The math over a typical 20-day month:

  • 18 dry days × +$10 saved = +$180
  • 2 rainy days × −$20 Ubered = −$40
  • Net: +$140 a month, every month.

Positive expected value. The math is solid. Walking is the right move.

Then Week 1 happens. It rains Monday. Rains Tuesday. Rains Wednesday. You've Ubered $60 in three days and the "math" says you should be ahead. You're not. You're down.

That's variance. The expected value didn't change — three rainy days in a row is just what randomness looks like at small samples. Some weeks you'll have zero rainy days and feel like a genius. Some weeks four and wonder if walking even works.

In the analogy In real betting
Average $7/day savingsYour bet's expected value
Dry day (save $10)A winning bet
Rainy day (lose $20)A losing bet

The takeaway: if your math is right, variance is just the cost of waiting for the math to work. Don't change your strategy because of a bad week. Change it only if your underlying math is wrong.

🪙 SAME IDEA, EVEN SIMPLER — THE +110 COIN FLIP

A friend offers you +110 on a fair coin flip, $10, once a day, every day. Win $11 on heads, lose $10 on tails. Do you take it?

Every single day, forever. It's +$0.50 of EV per flip — a permanent 5% edge on a pure 50/50.

But you'll still lose 4 flips in a row about once a month (1-in-16 per run). You'll be down $40 on a "sure thing" and it'll feel broken. It isn't — the coin has no memory, and the edge is exactly what it was on day one. After 100 flips there's still roughly a 1-in-3 chance you're down; after 1,000, about 1-in-15; keep going and it collapses toward zero.

You'd never cancel the deal because Tuesday through Friday went badly. That's the whole discipline: sports betting is the same game, except nobody tells you the coin is fair — finding out is what the devig and fair-price work is for.

Same sign, always — they're one number in two units

Here's a question worth asking before we go further: can a bet have a negative probability gap but still be +EV? Or a positive gap but negative EV?

No. It's mathematically impossible — and seeing why is the fastest way to really understand both metrics. Simplify the EV formula and you get:

The one-line proof
EV, simplifiedEV = your gap ÷ the win chance baked into the price
Example+5-point gap ÷ 50% price = +10% EV — same two numbers from above, one division
EV is literally just the gap divided by a positive number. Dividing can stretch or shrink it — it can never flip its sign. Negative gap → negative EV. Positive gap → positive EV. Every time, at every price.

So probability gap and EV aren't two metrics that happen to agree — they're the same edge expressed in two units. Gap is your edge in probability points. EV is that same edge converted into return-on-stake. And that division is exactly why the same gap explodes at long odds: dividing by 20% (a +400 dog) instead of 75% (a −300 favorite) makes the identical edge worth almost four times as much.

Sanity-check rule: if any tool, tout, or spreadsheet ever shows you +EV on a bet with a negative probability gap (or the reverse), the two numbers were measured against different baselines. Something's mismeasured — and the section below covers the three ways it happens.

Why they diverge — the payout multiplier

EV multiplies your probability edge by the payout. The bigger the payout per win, the bigger your edge converts into actual return.

A favorite at −300 pays 0.33 to 1. A dog at +400 pays 4 to 1. Same dollar staked, very different consequences when you win.

That's why the same probability gap produces different EV at different prices. Watch the same +5% gap travel across the price ladder:

Probability gap measures how wrong the book is. EV measures how much that being-wrong is worth to you.

The "looks the same, isn't" trap

Two bets land in your tracker the same week:

Bet A — coin-flip favorite
Market−110 (52.4% implied)
Your number (model / devig)57.4% true
Probability gap+5.0%
EV%+9.5%
Bet B — long-odds dog
Market+400 (20.0% implied)
Your number (model / devig)25.0% true
Probability gap+5.0%
EV%+25.0%

Identical probability gaps. Bet B is 2.6x more profitable per dollar wagered in expectation. If you're sizing both the same — flat 1 unit, say — you're underweighting the dog. If you're sizing by gap-only Kelly, same problem. Sizing has to use EV (or true probability + price together), not gap alone.

This is also why "5% edge" tools that only show one number can mislead. Always check whether that "edge" means gap or EV. Most reputable tools show both — or just EV — because EV is the one that maps to dollars.

When to use each one

  • Use probability gap when you're checking your own model accuracy. Did I project Player X correctly? The clean answer is in probability units — did I beat the market by 4 points or did I miss by 2? That's a gap question.
  • Use EV when you're deciding whether to bet, and how much. Bet sizing, comparing opportunities, ranking your daily card, calculating expected return on a bankroll — all EV. Always EV.
  • Use Kelly stake sizing on top of EV, not on gap alone. Kelly blends the same two ingredients — your win chance and the payout — into a stake size. Because the payout is baked in, the dog gets sized differently than the favorite even at the identical gap. Gap-only sizing can't do that.
Practical rule: if the number's about to influence a stake size or a "should I bet this?" decision, it had better be EV. If it's about checking whether your model is calibrated, gap is fine.

Common mistakes

  • "This bet has 5% edge" — edge in what? If the tool reports gap, the actual EV could be 3% on a heavy favorite or 25% on a long shot. Always know which number you're looking at.
  • Skipping dogs because the "edge looks the same." The most expensive habit in +EV betting. Long-shot edges are EV gold; gap-only thinking buries them.
  • Overweighting heavy favorites. A −400 favorite with a +3% gap is barely +3% EV. Sizing it like a +200 dog with the same gap is leaking money on every favorite.
  • Confusing EV% with hit rate. A +25% EV bet still loses 75% of the time at +400. The EV is the long-run average, not a per-game prediction. Variance still wins quarters, EV wins seasons.
  • Comparing two bets by gap. "Both have a 4% gap, I'll bet whichever I like more." No — bet whichever has more EV, every time. The gap is a diagnostic, not a decision metric.
  • Trusting monster longshot EV at face value. The same division that inflates dog EV also inflates your estimation errors. A 2-point mistake at −300 barely moves EV; at +1900 it's the difference between +40% and break-even. Real-world tests of naive odds-comparison at long prices have shown steady losses — books hide extra margin exactly where EV looks juiciest. Big dog EV deserves a bigger skepticism discount, not a bigger bet.

If a metric isn't priced in dollars, it's not telling you what the bet is worth. Probability gap is a diagnostic. EV is the answer.

"But I saw +EV with a negative gap" — the three illusions

We just proved the signs can never split. And yet people swear they've seen it — a screenshot showing +EV on a bet their model hates, or a positive gap that somehow prices out negative. Every single sighting is one of these three, and each one teaches you something real.

Illusion 1 — Baseline mismatch
What happenedThe gap was measured against the devigged sharp fair, but the EV was computed at your book's actual price. Two different implied probabilities — so the signs can point different ways.
ExampleSharp fair says 55%. Your model says 56% → gap +1% vs. fair. But your book is charging −140 (58.3% implied) → EV at that price is negative.
The edge existed at the fair price — your book's price ate it. Not a math contradiction; two different baselines. This is the entire reason line shopping matters: same opinion, and the bet is +EV at one book and −EV at another.
Illusion 2 — Devig method mismatch
What happenedFair probability isn't one number — it depends on how you strip the vig. Proportional, additive, and power devigs give slightly different fairs, and a borderline bet can be +EV under one and −EV under another.
Where it bitesA tool reports "gap" using one method and "EV%" using another — or a screenshot from one scanner disagrees with your own math.
Know what your tool is measuring. The Devig Calculator shows all three methods side by side — on thin margins, check whether the edge survives every method before you trust it.
Illusion 3 — Subsidized payouts (promos, boosts, bonus bets)
What happenedThe payout got changed, so the price paying you isn't the market price anymore. A bonus bet is +EV in dollars with zero probability edge — you're not risking your own cash. An odds boost can turn a market-fair bet +EV because the boosted price implies a lower probability than the market's.
ExampleBook boosts +100 → +130 on a fair coin flip. Your prob (50%) is below nothing — but measured against the boosted price (43.5% implied), the gap is +6.5% and the EV is positive.
Measured against the price that actually pays you, the signs match again. This is the whole engine behind bonus conversion: manufacturing +EV from the payout side, without ever needing a probability edge.

Measured against the price that actually pays you, the signs always match. If they don't, you're looking at two different baselines — find out which two.

One more wrinkle: vig vs. true edge

The standard mistake assumes the book's price equals their honest opinion. It doesn't — the price has vig baked in. The book's true opinion is the no-vig (fair) probability, not the implied probability.

To find your real probability gap, you should be comparing your true probability against the no-vig fair, not the raw implied. Otherwise the gap looks bigger than it is — because part of it is just the vig you'd be paying anyway.

Raw gap vs. no-vig gap
Market−110 / −110 (both sides)
Raw implied (over)52.4%
No-vig fair (over)50.0%vig stripped
Your number (model / devig)53.0%
Raw gap: 53.0 − 52.4 = +0.6% → looks like a tiny edge.
No-vig gap: 53.0 − 50.0 = +3.0% → the actual edge after accounting for vig.

EV calculations done off the raw market price already account for this — you'll be paying the vig on the bet, so it's correctly priced into your expected return. The no-vig benchmark is for measuring your opinion's edge against the market's honest opinion. Don't double-count the vig in either direction.
